Statement

A functor F:C→C′F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{C}'F:C→C′ is an equivalence of categories if there exist a functor G:C′→CG : \mathcal{C}' \to \mathcal{C}G:C′→C and natural isomorphisms

F∘G≅1C′,G∘F≅1CF \circ G \cong 1_{\mathcal{C}'}, \qquad G \circ F \cong 1_{\mathcal{C}}F∘G≅1C′​,G∘F≅1C​

one then writes C≃C′\mathcal{C} \simeq \mathcal{C}'C≃C′.

If the two ≅\cong≅ are strengthened to equalities F∘G=1C′F \circ G = 1_{\mathcal{C}'}F∘G=1C′​ and G∘F=1CG \circ F = 1_{\mathcal{C}}G∘F=1C​, it is called an isomorphism of categories, written C≅C′\mathcal{C} \cong \mathcal{C}'C≅C′.

Notes

An isomorphism requires the two composites to equal the identity functors; an equivalence only requires them to be naturally isomorphic to the identities. The latter is much weaker and much more useful: for instance, "finite-dimensional vector spaces" is equivalent to "matrices over F\mathbb{F}F", but can never be isomorphic (even the cardinalities of the object classes are wrong).

Under an equivalence, the question "are two categories the same" is replaced by "can they be translated into each other without losing structure" — every later statement in category theory that "C\mathcal{C}C and C′\mathcal{C}'C′ are the same" means this.
