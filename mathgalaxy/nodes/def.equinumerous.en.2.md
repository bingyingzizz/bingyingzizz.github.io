# equipotent　`def.equinumerous`
English entry · root `../`
[in graph](../#n=def.equinumerous)　·　[Chinese page (authoritative)](./def.equinumerous.md)

Classical results:
- $\mathbb{N}$ is equinumerous with $\mathbb{Q}$ and with $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ (both are countable);

- $(0, 1)$ is equinumerous with $\mathbb{R}$ (via something like $x \mapsto \tan \pi(x - 1/2)$);

- $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are **not** equinumerous (Cantor's theorem).

$A \subseteq B$ has nothing to do with equinumerosity: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Q}$ yet the two are equinumerous — this is precisely the dividing line between infinite and finite sets (Dedekind's characterization: equinumerous with a proper subset $\iff$ infinite).
