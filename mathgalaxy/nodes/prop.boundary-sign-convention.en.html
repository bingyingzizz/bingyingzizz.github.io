Statement

What this does: it aligns two "boundaries" — the algebraic boundary ∂c\partial c∂c determined on a cube by the coordinate numbering, and the geometric boundary of a manifold with boundary determined by "outward normal first". They differ by exactly the signs (−1)i+α(-1)^{i+\alpha}(−1)i+α.

Let MMM be an oriented nnn-dimensional manifold with boundary and c:In→Mc : I^{n}\to Mc:In→M an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism onto its image — InI^{n}In is mapped onto that image, which is a region with boundary and is nnn-dimensional, with InI^{n}In carrying the standard orientation, and suppose the boundary of the cube lands on ∂M\partial M∂M: c(∂In)⊆∂Mc(\partial I^{n}) \subseteq \partial Mc(∂In)⊆∂M. (⚠ This cannot be dropped: take M=RM = \mathbb{R}M=R and c(t)=tc(t) = tc(t)=t; then ∂M=∅\partial M = \emptyset∂M=∅, the "image of ∂M\partial M∂M" below is empty, and the whole proposition collapses.)

Then for every face (i,α)(i,\alpha)(i,α): viewing c(i,α)c_{(i,\alpha)}c(i,α)​ as a parametrization of a piece of ∂M\partial M∂M, it agrees with the induced orientation of ∂M\partial M∂M if and only if (−1)i+α=+1(-1)^{i+\alpha} = +1(−1)i+α=+1.

In other words: after reordering the signs of all faces according to the induced orientation, the resulting chain is

∂c=∑i=1n∑α=01(−1)i+α c(i,α).\partial c = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{1}(-1)^{i+\alpha}\,c_{(i,\alpha)} .∂c=i=1∑n​α=0∑1​(−1)i+αc(i,α)​.

So the "algebraic boundary" and the "geometric boundary" are not two conventions but one: the signs are already written in the coefficients of ∂c\partial c∂c.

Notes

Why this matters: the manifold version of Stokes' theorem uses the geometric boundary (induced orientation), while the chain version (链上的 Stokes 定理) uses the algebraic boundary (∂c\partial c∂c). That both can be written in one formula is entirely due to this proposition. It is also the true source of the "outward normal + induced orientation" sign in the divergence theorem.

Check for n=1n = 1n=1: I1=[0,1]I^{1} = [0,1]I1=[0,1] and ∂c=−c(1,0)+c(1,1)=c(1)−c(0)\partial c = -c_{(1,0)}+c_{(1,1)} = c(1)-c(0)∂c=−c(1,0)​+c(1,1)​=c(1)−c(0). In the induced orientation of dimension 000, the end x=1x = 1x=1 (outward normal +e1+e_{1}+e1​) is positive and the end x=0x = 0x=0 is negative. Term by term, they agree.

Check for n=2n = 2n=2: ∂c=−c(1,0)+c(1,1)+c(2,0)−c(2,1)\partial c = -c_{(1,0)}+c_{(1,1)}+c_{(2,0)}-c_{(2,1)}∂c=−c(1,0)​+c(1,1)​+c(2,0)​−c(2,1)​.

The four edges are left, right, bottom and top; the right edge (α=1\alpha = 1α=1, outward normal +e1+e_{1}+e1​) gets +++, the left edge (outward normal −e1-e_{1}−e1​) gets −-−, the bottom edge (outward normal −e2-e_{2}−e2​) gets +++, and the top edge (outward normal +e2+e_{2}+e2​) gets −-−.

Checking one by one with "outward normal first", all four agree — and the directions of the four edges run exactly once around counterclockwise.

⚠ The direction that is easy to copy wrong: the proof compares, in Rn\mathbb{R}^{n}Rn, "the determinant of the face's basis together with NNN", not "the direction of the cross product of the face's basis with NNN". The former is pure algebra and can be checked term by term; the latter is only intuitive in three dimensions. Intuition below dimension three helps one remember, but is not a proof.

⚠ Orientation preservation cannot be dropped: if ccc reverses orientation, every face gains an extra minus sign and the conclusion flips sign. Hence the hypothesis is "ccc orientation-preserving", not "ccc is a diffeomorphism".
