Statement

Let fff be differentiable on an interval III. Then fff is constant on III if and only if for every x∈Ix \in Ix∈I,

f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0f′(x)=0

Notes

⭐ This corollary means more than it seems: differentiation loses exactly one constant on an interval.

That is why "finding an antiderivative" makes sense and is determined only up to a constant (the other half of the fundamental theorem of calculus).

⚠ The domain must be an interval: on (0,1)∪(2,3)(0, 1) \cup (2, 3)(0,1)∪(2,3) a function whose derivative vanishes everywhere can take different constants on the two pieces.

⭐ In measure theory its status changes: f′=0f' = 0f′=0 a.e. does not force fff to be constant (the Cantor function) — "almost everywhere" is not enough to pin down a function, and absolute continuity is exactly the missing hypothesis.
