Statement

Let AAA be a unital ring and MMM an abelian group**. We say that MMM is a AAA-module** if there is a map

A×M⟶M,(a,m)⟼a⋅m,A \times M \longrightarrow M, \qquad (a, m) \longmapsto a \cdot m,A×M⟶M,(a,m)⟼a⋅m,

An abelian-group homomorphism preserving this action is called a **AAA-module homomorphism**: f(am)=af(m)f(am) = a f(m)f(am)=af(m). All AAA-modules with module homomorphisms form the category A-ModA\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}A-Mod.

Notes

⭐ A module = an abelian group + a ring action**, so it is a child of both "group" and "ring": a Z\mathbb{Z}Z-module is** an abelian group (n⋅mn \cdot mn⋅m admits only one definition); a module over a field **is** a vector space. These two statements place "module" midway between known examples.

**Why A-ModA\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}A-Mod is the model example of homological algebra**: it is simultaneously

  • an **abelian category** (it has kernels and cokernels, and all monomorphisms and epimorphisms are strict);
  • **with enough injective objects** (every module embeds into an injective module);
  • **with enough projective objects** (free modules are projective);
  • a **Grothendieck category** (it has a generator, AAA itself, and filtered colimits are exact).

So "what can be done in A-ModA\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}A-Mod" is exactly what homological algebra wants to generalize to arbitrary categories. Everything other categories can do is measured against it.
