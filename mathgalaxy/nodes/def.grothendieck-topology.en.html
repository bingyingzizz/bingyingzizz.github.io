Statement

A Grothendieck topology on a category C\mathcal{C}C assigns to each XXX a family of covering sieves J(X)J(X)J(X), subject to:

  3. Transitivity: for R,R′⊆hXR, R' \subseteq h_{X}R,R′⊆hX​, if R′∈J(X)R' \in J(X)R′∈J(X) and for every f∈R′(Y)f \in R'(Y)f∈R′(Y) one has f∗(R)∈J(Y)f^{*}(R) \in J(Y)f∗(R)∈J(Y), then R∈J(X)R \in J(X)R∈J(X).

A category with a Grothendieck topology is called a site.

Notes

J(X)J(X)J(X) is a filter in the poset of all sieves: nonempty; closed under intersections (R,R′∈J(X)  ⟹  R∩R′∈J(X)R, R' \in J(X) \implies R \cap R' \in J(X)R,R′∈J(X)⟹R∩R′∈J(X)); closed under larger sieves (R⊆R′R \subseteq R'R⊆R′, R∈J(X)  ⟹  R′∈J(X)R \in J(X) \implies R' \in J(X)R∈J(X)⟹R′∈J(X)).

Together these say that J(X)J(X)J(X) contains a bunch of "large enough" sieves.

A pretopology generates a topology: replace each covering family by the sieve it generates, then take all such sieves. Topologies are more general than pretopologies — a pretopology requires those fibre products to exist, whereas in a presheaf category pullbacks of sieves always exist.
