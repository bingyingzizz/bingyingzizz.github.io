Statement

A field KKK is algebraically closed if every nonconstant polynomial in K[x]K[x]K[x] factors into linear factors over K[x]K[x]K[x] (equivalently: every nonconstant f∈K[x]f \in K[x]f∈K[x] has a root in KKK).

If F‾/F\overline{F}/FF/F is algebraic and F‾\overline{F}F is algebraically closed, then F‾\overline{F}F is called an algebraic closure of FFF.

Notes

⭐ C\mathbb{C}C is algebraically closed — this is the fundamental theorem of algebra: C=R(i)\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)C=R(i) is the algebraic closure of R\mathbb{R}R. By contrast Q‾\overline{\mathbb{Q}}Q​ (the algebraic closure of Q\mathbb{Q}Q inside C\mathbb{C}C) is the standard example of an extension that is algebraic but infinite: this is exactly where “algebraic ≠\ne= finite” comes from.

⭐ Existence of algebraic closures needs choice: well-order the monic irreducibles over FFF, quotient by them one at a time, build a chain of extensions inductively, and take the union by Zorn's lemma (the union is again a field, and algebraic). So “every field has an algebraic closure” sits oddly in the graph: it is one of the rare existence theorems in field theory that need AC.

⚠ Algebraic closures are unique up to isomorphism (all are algebraic over FFF and algebraically closed), but not unique up to a unique isomorphism — for example the two embeddings of Q(2)\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})Q(2​) can land in two different places inside Q‾\overline{\mathbb{Q}}Q​.

📌 Relation to Galois theory: F‾/F\overline{F}/FF/F is in general not a finite extension, hence not a Galois extension (the fundamental theorem requires finite). Its “Galois group” is the profinite group Gal⁡(F‾/F)\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{F}/F)Gal(F/F), which calls for the infinite version of the theory.
