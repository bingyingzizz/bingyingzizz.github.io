Notes

⭐ This is the **end point** of the division algorithm: Euclidean domain   ⟹  \implies⟹ principal ideal domain   ⟹  \implies⟹ unique factorization domain.

All three "domain" definitions along the chain are worth remembering, because each has counterexamples in abstract algebra (a UFD need not be a PID: Z[x]\mathbb{Z}[x]Z[x];

a PID need not be a Euclidean domain: certain rings).

⭐ Uniqueness relies on "irreducible   ⟹  \implies⟹ prime": if ppp is irreducible and p∣ghp \mid ghp∣gh, then p∣gp \mid gp∣g or p∣hp \mid hp∣h.

This step uses the **Bézout identity** (a product of the Euclidean algorithm), and is precisely the only place where the division algorithm is really used.

⭐ Uses: factorization of integer-coefficient polynomials in Q[x]\mathbb{Q}[x]Q[x], the field structure of K[x]/(f)K[x]/(f)K[x]/(f), and the relation between characteristic and minimal polynomials (next batch) all rest on it.
