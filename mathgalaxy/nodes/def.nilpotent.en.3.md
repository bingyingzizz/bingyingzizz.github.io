## Notes · **draft**
**The standard example**: the shift operator $T(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) = (0, x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n-1})$, with $T^{n} = 0$ while $T^{n-1} \ne 0$. Its matrix is the "shift up by one" matrix, namely a single $J_{n}(0)$.

⭐ **Why nilpotent operators matter so much**: every operator splits as "diagonalizable part + nilpotent part" $T = S + N$ ($S$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent, $SN = NS$; this is the **Jordan–Chevalley decomposition**). So "understanding nilpotent" = "understanding all the difficulty of non-diagonalizability".
