thm.change-of-basis

Statement

Let T:V→VT : V \to VT:V→V be linear and let B,B′\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{B}'B,B′ be two ordered bases of VVV.

Write P:=[id]B′BP := [\mathrm{id}]_{\mathcal{B}'}^{\mathcal{B}}P:=[id]B′B​ (transition matrix; its jjj-th column is the coordinate vector of the B′\mathcal{B}'B′-th vector of jjj in the basis B\mathcal{B}B).

Then PPP is invertible, and

[T]B′=P−1 [T]B P[T]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1}\,[T]_{\mathcal{B}}\,P[T]B′​=P−1[T]B​P

In this case the two matrices are called similar (written A∼BA \sim BA∼B). Similarity is an equivalence relation.

Notes

⭐ In one sentence: changing basis is conjugation.

The P−1APP^{-1}APP−1AP in PPP governs "how coordinates change", and one factor entering and one leaving on the two sides exactly cancels the choice of basis — so "similar" is an intrinsic property of the matrix (belonging to the linear map), not a property of the array of numbers itself.

⭐ A division of labour worth remembering: AAA and BBB are similar   ⟺  \iff⟺ they are the matrices of one and the same linear map in two bases;

AAA and BBB are equivalent (only B=PAQB = PAQB=PAQ with P,QP, QP,Q invertible is required)   ⟺  \iff⟺ they have equal rank (even the spaces may change).

⚠ Similarity is much stronger than equivalence: similar matrices have the same rank, trace, determinant and characteristic polynomial — these are similarity invariants (needed later for the theory of eigenvalues).
