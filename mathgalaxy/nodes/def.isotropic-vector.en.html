Statement

What this is about: in a space with an inner product, ⟨v,v⟩=0\langle v,v\rangle = 0⟨v,v⟩=0 has only the zero solution. A general bilinear form has no such property — a nonzero vector whose "length" is zero is called an isotropic vector, and this is where this geometry parts ways with inner-product geometry.

Let β\betaβ be a symmetric bilinear form on VVV (or the corresponding quadratic form qqq). One says that v≠0v \ne 0v=0 is isotropic if

β(v,v)=0\beta(v, v) = 0β(v,v)=0 (that is, q(v)=0q(v) = 0q(v)=0)

Such a vvv is orthogonal to itself. If β\betaβ is nondegenerate and an isotropic vector exists, β\betaβ is called isotropic; if there is no nonzero isotropic vector it is called anisotropic.

Example: on R2\mathbb{R}^{2}R2, for β(x,y)=x1y1−x2y2\beta(x,y) = x_{1}y_{1} - x_{2}y_{2}β(x,y)=x1​y1​−x2​y2​, both (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1) are isotropic; the standard inner product has no isotropic vectors.

Notes

Geometric picture: think of q(x)=1q(x) = 1q(x)=1 as the "unit sphere". A positive definite form gives an ellipsoid (anisotropic); an indefinite form gives a hyperboloid, and the hyperboloid has two lines through the origin as asymptotes; those two lines are exactly the null cone q=0q = 0q=0 (not on the hyperboloid), and the nonzero vectors on it are the isotropic ones. So "isotropic" = "light-cone direction".

⚠️ The existence of isotropic vectors does not depend on the basis (q(v)=0q(v) = 0q(v)=0 is a coordinate-free statement), but whether they exist depends on the form: a real symmetric form is governed by its inertia indices — as soon as qqq is indefinite (both p,qp, qp,q nonzero) there are isotropic vectors; if it is positive or negative definite there are none.

⭐ In relativity this is exactly "light-like": take β\betaβ to be the Minkowski form, and isotropic vectors are the directions of light speed. Mathematically the same thing is called "null vector / isotropic vector", just under different names.

⚠️ Do not confuse "isotropic" with "zero vector": an isotropic vector must be nonzero; only its "squared length" is zero.
