Statement

Let FFF be a set and +++, ⋅\cdot⋅ two binary operations on FFF (that is, two functions F×F→FF \times F \to FF×F→F). Then (F,+,⋅)(F, +, \cdot)(F,+,⋅) is a field if and only if:

  • (F,+)(F, +)(F,+) is an abelian group;
  • (F∖{0},⋅)(F \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)(F∖{0},⋅) is an abelian group (here 000 is the identity of the additive group);
  • the distributive law a(b+c)=ab+aca (b + c) = ab + aca(b+c)=ab+ac holds for all a,b,c∈Fa, b, c \in Fa,b,c∈F.

In one sentence: addition, subtraction, multiplication and division (by non-zero elements) are all closed, and associativity, commutativity and distributivity hold.

Notes

⭐ The format is "set + operations + axioms": a field is not an object out of nowhere — one set, two functions and a few identities determine it.

To decide whether something is a field, check these identities one by one.

Note that the three items above cite the two axiom groups of a group directly: the definition of a field is one layer on top of the definition of a group, not a fresh enumeration of identities.

Constructions already available:

  • Q\mathbb{Q}Q (rationals): the handiest field;
  • R\mathbb{R}R (reals): after constructing it from Cauchy sequences, verify that it satisfies these identities (see "R\mathbb{R}R is a complete ordered field");
  • Z\mathbb{Z}Z is not a field: 222 has no multiplicative inverse in it;

N\mathbb{N}N is not either: 1−21 - 21−2 is meaningless.

⚠ "field" here is not the same as the "ring" of measure theory: there a ring is a ring of sets, meaning a family of sets closed under finite unions and differences — same name, different object.

Which hypothesis each direction uses (the compressed form of the field axioms relies on the two groups of group axioms):

  • field   ⟺  \iff⟺ additive abelian group, multiplicative abelian group on non-zero elements, and distributivity: unwinding the definition of this node; the non-zero elements forming a group also makes them non-empty, hence 0≠10\ne10=1.
  • field   ⟹  \implies⟹ division by non-zero elements and no zero divisors: multiplicative inverses give cancellation, and absorption of zero follows from distributivity and additive cancellation; no axiom of choice is needed.
  • non-zero commutative ring with identity in which every non-zero element is invertible   ⟹  \implies⟹ field: verify the group conditions directly; "non-zero" cannot be dropped, since the zero ring satisfies the conditions vacuously on non-zero elements yet is not a field.
  • integral domain   ⟹  \implies⟹ field fails in general, e.g. the integers; a finite integral domain does work, since multiplication by a non-zero element is injective and hence bijective on a finite set; finiteness is the key.
