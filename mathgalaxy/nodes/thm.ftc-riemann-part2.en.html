Statement

Let F:[a,b]→RF : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}F:[a,b]→R be differentiable and F′F'F′ integrable on [a,b][a, b][a,b]. Then

∫abF′(x) dx=F(b)−F(a)\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)∫ab​F′(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)

(FFF is called an antiderivative of F′F'F′; another way to write the formula is ∫abf=F∣ab\int_a^b f = F\bigr|_a^b∫ab​f=F​ab​.)

Notes

⭐ This is the main theorem of elementary calculus: computing areas becomes finding antiderivatives.

It also explains why tables of indefinite integrals are worth memorising — every derivative formula, read backwards, is an integration formula.

⚠ The hypothesis “F′F'F′ is integrable” cannot be dropped: there are differentiable functions whose derivative is not integrable (the F(x)=x2sin⁡(1/x2)F(x) = x^{2}\sin(1/x^{2})F(x)=x2sin(1/x2) type).

⚠ The converse is not strong enough either: Newton–Leibniz requires FFF to be an antiderivative;

the Lebesgue version (微积分基本定理（Lebesgue 版）) relaxes the hypothesis to absolute continuity and makes “F′F'F′ exists” part of the conclusion.

The relation between the two theorems is “the same statement in two integral settings”.
