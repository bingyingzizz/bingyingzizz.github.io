Statement

Let A\mathcal{A}A be an abelian category; both rows of the following commutative diagram are exact:

A→  f  B→  g  C⟶0a↓b↓c↓0⟶A′→  f′  B′→  g′  C′\begin{array}{ccccccc} A & \xrightarrow{\;f\;} & B & \xrightarrow{\;g\;} & C & \longrightarrow & 0 \\[2pt] {\scriptstyle a}\big\downarrow & & {\scriptstyle b}\big\downarrow & & {\scriptstyle c}\big\downarrow & & \\[2pt] 0 & \longrightarrow & A' & \xrightarrow[\;f'\;]{} & B' & \xrightarrow[\;g'\;]{} & C' \end{array}Aa↓⏐​0​f​⟶​Bb↓⏐​A′​g​f′​​Cc↓⏐​B′​⟶g′​​0C′​

Then there is a connecting homomorphism δ:ker⁡c→coker⁡a\delta : \ker c \to \operatorname{coker} aδ:kerc→cokera making the sequence

ker⁡a→ker⁡b→ker⁡c→  δ  coker⁡a→coker⁡b→coker⁡c\ker a \to \ker b \to \ker c \xrightarrow{\;\delta\;} \operatorname{coker} a \to \operatorname{coker} b \to \operatorname{coker} ckera→kerb→kercδ​cokera→cokerb→cokerc

exact.

Notes

Why "snake": draw this long exact sequence as a loop; the six groups/modules string together into the shape of a snake — δ\deltaδ is the turn at its head.

⚠️ One cannot chase elements. A\mathcal{A}A is a general abelian category; its objects have no "elements" to pick, so a phrase like x∈ker⁡cx \in \ker cx∈kerc is meaningless here. The legitimate substitute is generalized elements: "x∈Xx \in Xx∈X" is always read as "given any morphism t:T→Xt : T \to Xt:T→X" (Yoneda's element language), and everything else rests on the universal properties of kernels and cokernels.

⭐ Use: it is the engine of long exact sequences — stringing together each layer (kernel, cokernel) of a short exact sequence 0→K→L→M→00 \to K \to L \to M \to 00→K→L→M→0 uses the δ\deltaδ of each layer. Wherever one "has a short exact sequence and wants a long one", the snake lemma is running underneath.
