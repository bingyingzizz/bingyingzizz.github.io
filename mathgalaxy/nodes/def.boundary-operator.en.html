Statement

What it does: "take one face of a cube" has a uniform notation: fix one coordinate at this end or that end, multiply by a sign, and add everything up to get the boundary.

Notes

Where the sign (−1)i+α(-1)^{i+\alpha}(−1)i+α comes from: it is not forced.

Take the end whose outward normal is +∂i+\partial_{i}+∂i​ (that is, α=1\alpha = 1α=1) and the sign (−1)i+1(-1)^{i+1}(−1)i+1; the other end has normal −∂i-\partial_{i}−∂i​ and sign (−1)i(-1)^{i}(−1)i; the difference between the two ends is exactly the sign of the permutation "moving the outward normal to the front crosses i−1i-1i−1 coordinates".

The full verification is in 边界的符号约定 — the conclusion is that these signs agree face by face with the induced orientation "outward normal first".

2k2k2k faces: iii ranges over kkk values and α\alphaα over the two ends, so there are 2k2k2k (k−1)(k-1)(k−1)-cubes. They are not pairwise distinct as maps, but as terms of a chain they are pairwise distinct (they may coincide elsewhere, but not as maps).

Square zero: see 边界算子平方为零. This is the key to the whole bookkeeping — it guarantees "the boundary has no boundary", so Stokes' theorem does not acquire an extra layer on either side.

Why linear extension rather than term-by-term definition: a chain is already a formal sum; if ∂\partial∂ were not a homomorphism one could not compute ∂(c1+c2)\partial(c_{1}+c_{2})∂(c1​+c2​), and Stokes' theorem for chains requires exactly this.

⚠️ Notation trap: ∂\partial∂ is used simultaneously for the boundary of a half-space (∂Hn\partial\mathbb{H}^{n}∂Hn), the boundary of a manifold (∂M\partial M∂M), and the boundary of a chain (∂c\partial c∂c). The three uses are unified in 边界的符号约定: for orientation-preserving cubes, the chain boundary and the manifold boundary agree face by face.
