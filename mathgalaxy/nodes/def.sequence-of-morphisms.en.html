Statement

Let C\mathcal{C}C be a category. A (long) sequence in C\mathcal{C}C is a commutative diagram over the ordered set (Z,≤)(\mathbb{Z}, \le)(Z,≤), that is, a functor K:(Z,≤)→CK : (\mathbb{Z}, \le) \to \mathcal{C}K:(Z,≤)→C; written out, it is objects and morphisms

⋯⟶Kn−1→dn−1Kn→dnKn+1⟶⋯\cdots \longrightarrow K^{n-1} \xrightarrow{d^{n-1}} K^{n} \xrightarrow{d^{n}} K^{n+1} \longrightarrow \cdots⋯⟶Kn−1dn−1​Kndn​Kn+1⟶⋯

Since between any two objects of (Z,≤)(\mathbb{Z}, \le)(Z,≤) there is at most one morphism, commutativity is automatic: dm−1∘⋯∘dnd^{m-1} \circ \cdots \circ d^{n}dm−1∘⋯∘dn is the unique morphism Kn→KmK^{n} \to K^{m}Kn→Km. It is also called a sequence of morphisms.

Notes

A sequence does not require dn∘dn−1=0d^{n} \circ d^{n-1} = 0dn∘dn−1=0; adding this condition gives a (co)chain complex (see @@def.cochain-complex@@).

Example: a sequence in Ab\mathbf{Ab}Ab is a string of group homomorphisms; for C=Vectk\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Vect}_{k}C=Vectk​ it is a string of linear maps.

Finite sequences (only finitely many nonzero terms) and one-sided sequences (given only for n≥0n \ge 0n≥0, with values KnK^{n}Kn) are common variants; by default this graph uses two-sided infinite sequences, since complexes in homological algebra are usually indexed in both directions.
