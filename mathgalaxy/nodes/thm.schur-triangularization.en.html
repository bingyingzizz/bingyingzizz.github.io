Statement

What this does: Unitary diagonalization is available only for normal matrices (Spectral Theorem (Matrices)). If the requirement is relaxed to upper triangular form, every complex square matrix qualifies.

Let A∈Mn(C)A \in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})A∈Mn​(C). There is a unitary matrix UUU such that

U∗AU=T,U^{*}AU = T ,U∗AU=T,

where TTT is upper triangular and the diagonal entries of TTT are exactly the eigenvalues of AAA (in any prescribed order).

Real case: Every real square matrix is orthogonally similar to a real Schur form, which is block upper triangular with diagonal blocks of size 1×11\times 11×1 or 2×22\times 22×2 (the latter corresponding to conjugate pairs of complex eigenvalues).

Special case: When AAA is normal, TTT must be diagonal. This is the spectral theorem (Spectral Theorem (Matrices)).

Note on the real case: Nonreal eigenvalues occur in conjugate pairs. Taking real and imaginary parts gives an invariant two-dimensional real subspace. The same induction gives the real Schur form (with diagonal block sizes 1×11\times 11×1 or 2×22\times 22×2). The details are omitted. ∎

⭐ Why it is used more often than Jordan normal form: Jordan form uses arbitrary similarity transformations (P−1APP^{-1}APP−1AP). It is useful in theory, but PPP may be too ill-conditioned for computation. Schur form uses unitary similarity (U∗AUU^{*}AUU∗AU), whose condition number is always 1. Thus numerical linear algebra uses Schur form instead of Jordan form.

A shortcut to the spectral theorem: If AAA is normal and U∗AU=TU^{*}AU = TU∗AU=T is upper triangular, then TTT is also normal. An upper triangular normal matrix   ⟹  \implies⟹ must be diagonal (compare the two sides of (TT∗)11(TT^{*})_{11}(TT∗)11​ to see that the off-diagonal entries of the first row are zero, then use induction).

⚠️ In the real case, one cannot always obtain a real diagonal or triangular form with only 1×11\times11×1 blocks. The rotation example 90∘90^\circ90∘ shows why 2×22\times 22×2 blocks are necessary.
