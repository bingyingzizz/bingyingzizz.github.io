Notes

⭐ In one sentence: the group ring spreads group-theoretic problems into linear algebra — the multiplication of the group is expanded into a bilinear operation on RRR-modules, so the machinery of "modules", "resolutions", and "derived functors" becomes available for groups.

Basic properties:

  • when G={1}G = \{1\}G={1}, R[G]=RR[G] = RR[G]=R; when GGG is finite, R[G]R[G]R[G] is a free RRR-module with basis GGG, i.e. R[G]≅R⊕∣G∣R[G] \cong R^{\oplus |G|}R[G]≅R⊕∣G∣.
  • R[G]R[G]R[G] is in general not commutative: when gh≠hggh \ne hggh=hg, ggg and hhh are distinct basis elements, and ghg hgh and hgh ghg differ accordingly. So R[G]-ModR[G]\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}R[G]-Mod is in general not a symmetric monoidal category.
  • g↦g−1g \mapsto g^{-1}g↦g−1 gives R[G]op≅R[G]R[G]^{\mathrm{op}} \cong R[G]R[G]op≅R[G]. Thus "left modules" and "right modules" differ only by taking inverses — in group cohomology one need not worry much about left versus right.

Why it must be built: the group GGG itself is not a ring, so modules, injectives, and resolutions make no sense. After embedding GGG into R[G]R[G]R[G], "the action of GGG on some abelian group" becomes "a module over R[G]R[G]R[G]", and the whole of homological algebra applies at once.
