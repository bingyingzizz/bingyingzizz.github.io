Statement

A morphism i:Y→Xi : Y \to Xi:Y→X is called a regular monomorphism if it is the equalizer of a pair of parallel morphisms f,g:X⇉Zf, g : X \rightrightarrows Zf,g:X⇉Z (see @@def.equalizer@@):

i=ker⁡(f,g)  :  Y⟶X.i = \ker(f, g) \;:\; Y \longrightarrow X .i=ker(f,g):Y⟶X.

If the category has a zero object (such as Ab\mathbf{Ab}Ab), this is equivalent to "iii is the kernel of some morphism": ker⁡(f)=ker⁡(f,0)\ker(f) = \ker(f, 0)ker(f)=ker(f,0).

Notes

Example: in Set\mathbf{Set}Set, the regular monomorphisms are exactly the injective maps. Given an injection i:Y→Xi : Y \to Xi:Y→X, take Z={0,1}Z = \{0, 1\}Z={0,1}, let fff be defined by: on i(Y)i(Y)i(Y) it equals 000, on the complement it equals 111, and let ggg be constantly 000; then ker⁡(f,g)=i(Y)≅Y\ker(f, g) = i(Y) \cong Yker(f,g)=i(Y)≅Y.

Relation to monomorphisms: a regular monomorphism is always a monomorphism (an equalizer is monic), but not conversely. In Top\mathbf{Top}Top monomorphisms are injective continuous maps, whereas regular monomorphisms require the subspace topology to be exactly the one given by the equalizer, so not all are regular.

In Ab\mathbf{Ab}Ab: monomorphisms, regular monomorphisms, and kernels of some map all coincide; this is also the origin of the term "kernel".
