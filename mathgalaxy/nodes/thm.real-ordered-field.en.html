Statement

The (R,+,⋅,≤)(\mathbb{R}, +, \cdot, \le)(R,+,⋅,≤) constructed above satisfies three groups of properties:

(i) Field axioms: (R,+,⋅)(\mathbb{R}, +, \cdot)(R,+,⋅) is a field — closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication and division (by nonzero elements), with associative, commutative and distributive laws;

(ii) Order axioms: ≤\le≤ is a total order compatible with the operations:

a≤b  ⟹  a+c≤b+c,a≤b, 0≤c  ⟹  ac≤bca \le b \implies a + c \le b + c, \qquad a \le b,\ 0 \le c \implies ac \le bca≤b⟹a+c≤b+c,a≤b, 0≤c⟹ac≤bc

(iii) Completeness (least upper bound principle): every nonempty subset of R\mathbb{R}R that is bounded above has a least upper bound.

Moreover the embedded image of Q\mathbb{Q}Q is dense in R\mathbb{R}R.

Notes

⚠ Note where this statement sits: it is a theorem, not a definition — first comes the construction ("the real number system R\mathbb{R}R"), then the proof that what was constructed has these three groups of properties.

⭐ Item (iii) is the engine of analysis: without it Q\mathbb{Q}Q also satisfies the first two items, but {x∈Q:x2<2}\{ x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2 \}{x∈Q:x2<2} has no least upper bound in Q\mathbb{Q}Q.

(Q\mathbb{Q}Q is therefore not a complete ordered field — this is not a defect, but precisely the reason to complete Q\mathbb{Q}Q into R\mathbb{R}R.)

From (iii) follow the two most-used facts in analysis:

  • monotone bounded sequences converge;
  • the nested interval theorem, Bolzano–Weierstrass, and convergence of Cauchy sequences.

⚠ Completeness says "a least upper bound exists", not "a limit necessarily exists" — the latter is a corollary.

📌 The route to (iii) has two steps (see the proof on the edge): first prove that every Cauchy sequence in R\mathbb{R}R converges (extract a rational Cauchy sequence from the representatives by a diagonal argument), then derive the least upper bound principle from Cauchy completeness (bisection produces an increasing sequence and a decreasing sequence of upper bounds, squeezed to the same limit).

📌 Properties derived from these three items and used everywhere later: the Archimedean property and the density of Q\mathbb{Q}Q in R\mathbb{R}R (see the entry on the Archimedean property);

R\mathbb{R}R is also uncountable (#R=2ℵ0\#\mathbb{R} = 2^{\aleph_0}#R=2ℵ0​) — "dense" and "equinumerous" are two different things.
