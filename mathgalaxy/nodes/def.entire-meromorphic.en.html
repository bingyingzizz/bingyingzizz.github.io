Statement

In plain words: an entire function is one holomorphic everywhere on the whole complex plane; a meromorphic function is one "holomorphic except at a bunch of isolated poles", that is, locally a quotient of two holomorphic functions.

Entire function: fff is holomorphic everywhere on C\mathbb{C}C. By Liouville's theorem, a bounded entire function is constant; a non-constant entire function has a singularity at ∞\infty∞ (a polynomial has a pole there, while eze^{z}ez has an essential singularity).

Meromorphic function: let Ω\OmegaΩ be a region. If fff is holomorphic on Ω\OmegaΩ except on an isolated set PPP, and every point of PPP is a pole of fff, one says that fff is meromorphic on Ω\OmegaΩ.

Equivalent description: for every a∈Ωa \in \Omegaa∈Ω there are a neighbourhood UUU of aaa and holomorphic functions p,qp, qp,q on UUU (with q≢0q \not\equiv 0q≡0), such that f=p/qf = p/qf=p/q holds on UUU.

Notes

⭐ The sphere viewpoint is cleanest: replacing Ω\OmegaΩ by C^\hat{\mathbb{C}}C^, meromorphic functions are exactly the holomorphic maps C^→C^\hat{\mathbb{C}} \to \hat{\mathbb{C}}C^→C^ — poles are the points mapped to ∞\infty∞. The meromorphic functions on C^\hat{\mathbb{C}}C^ are precisely the rational functions.

⭐ Examples: 1/sin⁡z1/\sin z1/sinz (poles at kπk\pikπ), tan⁡z\tan ztanz, and the Γ\GammaΓ function (poles at 0,−1,−2,…0, -1, -2, \ldots0,−1,−2,…).

⚠ An essential singularity does not count as meromorphic: the behaviour of e1/ze^{1/z}e1/z near 000 is not a pole, so e1/ze^{1/z}e1/z is not meromorphic on a region containing 000. Meromorphic requires every bad point to be a pole.

⚠ "Isolated" cannot be dropped either: if the singularities have an accumulation point, it is not a pole.

📌 The Laurent expansion at a pole has only finitely many negative-power terms; near a pole a meromorphic function is "a blow-up of finite order". Entire functions are the special case P=∅P = \emptysetP=∅.
