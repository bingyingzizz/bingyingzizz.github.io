Statement

Let C\mathcal{C}C denote the set of all rational Cauchy sequences, and ∼\sim∼ the equivalence relation "difference is a null sequence" (see "Cauchy sequences and null sequences"). Put

R:=C/∼\mathbb{R} := \mathcal{C} / \simR:=C/∼

(The general order is given by [yn]≥[xn]:  ⟺  [yn−xn]≥0[y_n] \ge [x_n] :\iff [y_n - x_n] \ge 0[yn​]≥[xn​]:⟺[yn​−xn​]≥0.) Zero and one are the constant sequences:

q↦[(q,q,q,…)]q \mapsto [(q, q, q, \ldots)]q↦[(q,q,q,…)]

Notes

⭐ This is the construction: R\mathbb{R}R is not assumed to exist; it is glued together from objects of the previous level — the rationals (Q\mathbb{Q}Q) →\to→ rational sequences (functions ω→Q\omega \to \mathbb{Q}ω→Q) →\to→ the Cauchy sequences among them →\to→ passing to the quotient by the equivalence relation.

Every step uses only what is already available; no new object enters from nothing.

  • The elements are equivalence classes, not sequences themselves;

otherwise different ways of approximating the same number would become different real numbers.

  • Operations and order are written using representatives, so one must verify that they are independent of the choice of representative (well-defined) — the step most easily forgotten in a quotient construction.

⭐ The generalization of this device is "completion of a metric space": replace Q\mathbb{Q}Q by an arbitrary metric space (X,d)(X, d)(X,d), replace ∣xm−xn∣|x_m - x_n|∣xm​−xn​∣ by d(xm,xn)d(x_m, x_n)d(xm​,xn​), and "take Cauchy sequences, modulo null sequences" carries over verbatim — the construction of R\mathbb{R}R is the prototype.

📌 Three groups of properties are not in the definition: the field axioms, the order axioms, and the least-upper-bound principle are all theorems about this construction (see "R\mathbb{R}R is a complete ordered field"). Existence is given by the construction and needs no axioms.

📌 Another route is Dedekind cuts (taking subsets of Q\mathbb{Q}Q that are non-empty, bounded above, and have no greatest element): the routes differ, but the objects constructed are isomorphic — see "uniqueness of the complete ordered field".
