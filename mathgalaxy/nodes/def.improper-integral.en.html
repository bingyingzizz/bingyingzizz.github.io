Statement

Infinite interval: if fff is integrable on every [a,R][a, R][a,R], define

∫a+∞f:=lim⁡R→+∞∫aRf\int_a^{+\infty} f := \lim_{R \to +\infty} \int_a^{R} f∫a+∞​f:=R→+∞lim​∫aR​f

(called convergent when the limit exists and is finite, divergent otherwise).

Unbounded function: if fff is unbounded on [a,b][a, b][a,b] but integrable on every [a,b−ε][a, b - \varepsilon][a,b−ε], define

∫abf:=lim⁡ε→0+∫ab−εf\int_a^{b} f := \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^{+}} \int_a^{b - \varepsilon} f∫ab​f:=ε→0+lim​∫ab−ε​f

The two cases may mix (an infinite interval together with unboundedness), and then one must split: every piece must converge.

Notes

⭐ The improper integral is not a new theory: it is only a limit of Riemann integrals — every integral in the definition is an ordinary one.

⚠ Splitting is necessary in the mixed case: defining ∫−∞+∞f\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f∫−∞+∞​f as lim⁡R→∞∫−RR\lim_{R \to \infty}\int_{-R}^{R}limR→∞​∫−RR​ loses phenomena that are not absolutely convergent (∫−RRx dx=0\int_{-R}^{R} x\,dx = 0∫−RR​xdx=0 for every RRR, while both ∫0+∞\int_0^{+\infty}∫0+∞​ and ∫−∞0\int_{-\infty}^{0}∫−∞0​ diverge).

This distinction is called the principal value.
