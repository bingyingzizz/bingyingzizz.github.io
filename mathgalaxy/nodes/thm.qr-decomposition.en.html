Statement

What this does: Expresses Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization as a product of matrices. Denote the result by RRR and the orthogonalized basis by QQQ to obtain A=QRA = QRA=QR.

Suppose the columns of A∈Mm×n(R)A \in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})A∈Mm×n​(R) are linearly independent (m≥nm \ge nm≥n). There is then a unique factorization

A=QR,A = QR ,A=QR,

where Q∈Mm×nQ \in M_{m\times n}Q∈Mm×n​ has orthonormal columns (QTQ=InQ^{T}Q = I_{n}QTQ=In​) and R∈Mn(R)R \in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})R∈Mn​(R) is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries.

Complex case: A=QRA = QRA=QR, Q∗Q=IQ^{*}Q = IQ∗Q=I, and RRR is upper triangular (with positive real diagonal entries).

Square case: When AAA is invertible, QQQ is orthogonal (unitary). This gives the factorization of AAA into ‘orthogonal ×\times× upper triangular’ form, corresponding to the ‘lower triangular ×\times× upper triangular’ factorization in LU.

Notes

This is Gram–Schmidt: Orthogonalize the columns of AAA, denoted by a1,…,ana_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}a1​,…,an​, to obtain q1,…,qnq_{1}, \ldots, q_{n}q1​,…,qn​ (Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation). The only difference is that QR usually requires a positive diagonal for RRR (fixing the signs of qkq_kqk​).

⭐ Why use QR instead of Gram–Schmidt: They are mathematically equivalent but differ numerically. Gram–Schmidt can lose orthogonality (QTQQ^{T}QQTQ departs from III), so practical algorithms use Householder reflections or Givens rotations to compute QQQ directly. Only the mathematical results are covered here (numerical algorithms are outside the scope).

This is why QR is widely used in numerical computation.

⚠️ The linear independence of the columns is essential. Without it, QQQ has an incomplete set of columns and RRR is singular. A factorization is still possible but loses its usefulness in this form (use column-pivoted QR or a pseudoinverse in that case).
