Statement

In plain terms: something counterintuitive — "continuous integer-valued functions on SSS" look like "infinitely many copies of Z\mathbb{Z}Z glued together", yet they are always free abelian groups (a basis, no relations). This is the nail that holds up the whole theory of solid groups.

For every profinite set SSS, the group C(S,Z)C(S, \mathbb{Z})C(S,Z) of continuous functions (see 整值测度),

C(S,Z)={ f:S→Z ∣fcontinuous }C(S, \mathbb{Z}) = \{\, f : S \to \mathbb{Z}\ \mid f \text{continuous}\, \}C(S,Z)={f:S→Z ∣fcontinuous}

is a free abelian group.

Notes

⭐ This is a theorem of Specker (1950, for SSS of type Zp\mathbb{Z}_pZp​) and of Nöbeling (1968, for general profinite sets).

The counterintuitive point: C(S,Z)C(S,\mathbb{Z})C(S,Z) looks like the infinite product "one integer at each point", yet it is always free — while its dual M(S,Z)=∏IZM(S,\mathbb{Z}) = \prod_I \mathbb{Z}M(S,Z)=∏I​Z is the large side.

⭐ It is the pivot of the whole solid theory: without it, Z[S]■Z[S]^{\blacksquare}Z[S]■ could not be described as ∏IZ\prod_I \mathbb{Z}∏I​Z, and the "compact projective generators" in 固态群构成阿贝尔子范畴 would have no basis.

⚠ Note that C(S,Z)C(S,\mathbb{Z})C(S,Z) behaves completely differently from its bounded version and from C(S,R)C(S,\mathbb{R})C(S,R): the claim here concerns C(S,Z)C(S,\mathbb{Z})C(S,Z) as an abstract abelian group; C(S,R)C(S,\mathbb{R})C(S,R) as an abstract vector space of course has a basis, but the freeness of the former cannot simply be transferred.
