Statement

What this does: Gives the earlier counterpart of the Poisson bracket (Lagrange 1808, Poisson 1809), essentially the inverse of the Poisson bracket matrix. It measures the area between two families of curves, making it natural in the theory of canonical transformations.

Notes

Why the Lagrange bracket came first: The calculus of variations and the study of canonical transformations, as in Lagrange's Analytical Mechanics, preceded the concept of a symplectic manifold.

Poisson later found that the dual bracket satisfies the Jacobi identity, and it became central to the modern formulation.

The two brackets encode the same information (by inversion), but the Lagrange bracket does not satisfy the Jacobi identity and is now used as a computational tool.

⭐ Geometric meaning: The Lagrange bracket measures the area enclosed in phase space by the families of level curves of uuu and vvv. The Lagrange bracket thus describes areas, while the Poisson bracket describes flows. This is why the canonical transformation condition is more intuitive in terms of Lagrange brackets: it directly expresses area preservation.

⚠️ L=−P−1L = -P^{-1}L=−P−1 holds only when the comparison is made in the same coordinate system: Both matrices must be written using the same (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) as a basis to make the comparison meaningful. This is the easiest point to misremember.

⚠️ Notation: Some books write the Lagrange bracket as {u,v}\{u,v\}{u,v} (the same notation as for the Poisson bracket), while others write [u,v]q,p[u,v]_{q,p}[u,v]q,p​. We use the latter, with a subscript indicating the reference coordinates.
