Notes

⭐ The one-dimensional mean value theorem rests on Rolle, and Rolle on "a continuous function on a closed interval attains its extrema" — and multivariable calculus has no such thing (the equality version is false for Rm\mathbb{R}^{m}Rm-valued functions: f(t)=(cos⁡t,sin⁡t)f(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)f(t)=(cost,sint) goes once around a circle, f(2π)−f(0)=0f(2\pi) - f(0) = 0f(2π)−f(0)=0, yet DfDfDf is nowhere zero and ∥Df∥=1\|Df\| = 1∥Df∥=1).

⭐ So what is really useful in several variables is the inequality version: it needs only "apply the one-variable mean value theorem along a line segment + estimate the norm", and no existence of extrema.

The contraction mapping principle and the inverse function theorem later use nothing but this.

⚠ In the scalar case (fff has values in R\mathbb{R}R) the equality version needs care: the usual proof applies the one-dimensional MVT to φ(t)=f(a+th)\varphi(t) = f(a + th)φ(t)=f(a+th) and yields "there exists θ\thetaθ with f(a+h)−f(a)=Df(a+θh)(h)f(a+h) - f(a) = Df(a + \theta h)(h)f(a+h)−f(a)=Df(a+θh)(h)" — here θ\thetaθ depends on hhh and cannot be treated as a constant.
