Statement

Let X⊆RX \subseteq \mathbb{R}X⊆R, f:X→Rf : X \to \mathbb{R}f:X→R. Then fff is uniformly continuous if and only if

∀ε>0, ∃δ>0, ∀x,x′∈X: ∣x−x′∣<δ  ⟹  ∣f(x)−f(x′)∣<ε\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists \delta > 0,\ \forall x, x' \in X :\ |x - x'| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(x')| < \varepsilon∀ε>0, ∃δ>0, ∀x,x′∈X: ∣x−x′∣<δ⟹∣f(x)−f(x′)∣<ε

The only difference from "continuous" is the order of the quantifiers: here δ\deltaδ appears before ∀x\forall x∀x, so one and the same δ\deltaδ must work simultaneously for all points of XXX.

Notes

⭐ In one sentence: continuity gives "a δ\deltaδ for each point", uniform continuity gives "one δ\deltaδ that works for all of XXX".

⚠ The two are not equivalent: f(x)=x2f(x) = x^{2}f(x)=x2 is continuous on R\mathbb{R}R, but for x=nx = nx=n, x′=n+1/nx' = n + 1/nx′=n+1/n one has ∣f(x)−f(x′)∣=2+1/n2>2|f(x) - f(x')| = 2 + 1/n^{2} > 2∣f(x)−f(x′)∣=2+1/n2>2, which no δ\deltaδ, however small, can avoid — so it is not uniformly continuous.

f(x)=1/xf(x) = 1/xf(x)=1/x on (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) is the same.

⭐ On compact sets the two coincide (Cantor's theorem) — this is why elementary calculus can talk only about continuity and still use uniform-continuity properties freely.

⭐ Uniform continuity is the passport for everything that later "moves limits into integrals / derivatives": only uniformly convergent sequences of functions preserve continuity and preserve integrals.
