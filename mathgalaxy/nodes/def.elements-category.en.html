Statement

For a functor F:C→SetF : \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}F:C→Set, its category of elements el⁡(F)\operatorname{el}(F)el(F) has as objects

{(Y,t):Y∈C, t∈F(Y)}\bigl\{(Y, t) : Y \in \mathcal{C},\ t \in F(Y)\bigr\}{(Y,t):Y∈C, t∈F(Y)}

and as morphisms from (Y,t)(Y, t)(Y,t) to (Z,u)(Z, u)(Z,u) those arrows such that F(f)(t)=uF(f)(t) = uF(f)(t)=u, with f:Y→Zf : Y \to Zf:Y→Z the arrow itself.

One calls (X,s)(X, s)(X,s) a universal element of FFF if it is an initial object of el⁡(F)\operatorname{el}(F)el(F).

Notes

This explains the word "universal" once and for all: universal = an initial (or terminal) object in some auxiliary category. Thus "universal cone", "universal factorization" and "universal element" are three applications of the same slogan.

Examples: Z\mathbb{Z}Z is the initial object of the category of rings, so it is "universal among all rings"; the universal cone on the empty diagram is the terminal object; a limit is the terminal object of the category of cones.
