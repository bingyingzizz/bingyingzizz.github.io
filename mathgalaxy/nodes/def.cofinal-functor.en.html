Statement

Let F:J→IF : J \to IF:J→I be a functor. For i∈Ii \in Ii∈I, write i↓Fi \downarrow Fi↓F for the comma category whose objects are { f:i→F(j) }\{\, f : i \to F(j) \,\}{f:i→F(j)} (j∈Jj \in Jj∈J).

One says that FFF is cofinal (also written final) if for every i∈Ii \in Ii∈I:

i↓Fi \downarrow Fi↓F is non-empty and connected.

Notes

⭐ Intuition: the image of FFF in III is "everywhere present and not disconnected". Non-empty = every iii has a path into the image of FFF; connected = different paths can be converted into one another. With these two, JJJ "fills III densely enough", and taking a colimit along it is the same as along all of III (see the next item).

Dual: replacing i↓Fi \downarrow Fi↓F by the lower comma category F↓iF \downarrow iF↓i (objects are { f:F(j)→i }\{\, f : F(j) \to i \,\}{f:F(j)→i}) gives the condition called coinitial, which preserves limits.

Examples: J→1J \to \mathbf{1}J→1 is cofinal in   ⟺  \iff⟺ JJJ connected; the inclusion of a directed set along ≤\le≤, N→Z\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}N→Z, is cofinal; the inclusion of C\mathcal{C}C into its reflective subcategory is in general not cofinal (reversing the direction gives coinitial).
