Statement

Let XXX be a set and T⊆P(X)\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)T⊆P(X) a family of subsets of XXX. Then (X,T)(X, \mathcal{T})(X,T) is a topological space** and T\mathcal{T}T is a topology** on XXX iff:

  1. **Both ends**: ∅∈T\emptyset \in \mathcal{T}∅∈T and X∈TX \in \mathcal{T}X∈T;
  2. **Closed under arbitrary unions**: {Ui}i∈I⊆T  ⟹  ⋃i∈IUi∈T\{ U_i \}_{i \in I} \subseteq \mathcal{T} \implies \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \in \mathcal{T}{Ui​}i∈I​⊆T⟹⋃i∈I​Ui​∈T (III is arbitrary, possibly uncountable);
  3. **Closed under finite intersections**: U,V∈T  ⟹  U∩V∈TU, V \in \mathcal{T} \implies U \cap V \in \mathcal{T}U,V∈T⟹U∩V∈T.

The members of T\mathcal{T}T are called open sets**, and their complements closed sets**.

Notes

The three axioms abstract the properties of open intervals on the real line: a union of arbitrarily many open sets is open, but only **finitely** many open sets are guaranteed to have an open intersection (⋂n(−1/n,1/n)={0}\bigcap_n (-1/n, 1/n) = \{0\}⋂n​(−1/n,1/n)={0} is not open).

The advantage of this language is that **only the question "which sets count as open" remains**, so distances, metrics and coordinates can all be thrown away — continuity, compactness, connectedness and limits can still be expressed.

A set can carry several different topologies;

they are compared as "finer / coarser", not by size.

**Which assumptions each direction uses** (a topology is defined by axioms and imposes no metric or separation requirement):

  • topology   ⟺  \iff⟺ the three listed axioms: both directions unfold the definition and need no compactness, Choice or metric.
  • closure under arbitrary unions   ⟹  ∅\implies\emptyset⟹∅ is open requires allowing the empty index family; closure under binary intersections   ⟹  \implies⟹ closure under non-empty finite intersections follows by induction, while the empty intersection being XXX still needs the convention that XXX is open.
  • the open-set axioms   ⟺  \iff⟺ the family of complements satisfies arbitrary intersections and finite unions: use De Morgan's identities; no extra topological assumption is needed.
  • finite intersections cannot be upgraded to arbitrary ones: in the usual topology on the reals ⋂n≥1(−1/n,1/n)={0}\bigcap_{n\ge1}(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\}⋂n≥1​(−1/n,1/n)={0} is not open; nor is a topology automatically Hausdorff — the indiscrete topology on a set with more than one point is a counterexample.
