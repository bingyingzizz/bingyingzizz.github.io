Abstract

For a group homomorphism φ:G→G′\varphi : G \to G'φ:G→G′, the map gker⁡φ↦φ(g)g\ker\varphi \mapsto \varphi(g)gkerφ↦φ(g) is an isomorphism G/ker⁡φ≅im⁡φG/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphiG/kerφ≅imφ; in particular a surjective homomorphism has G/ker⁡φ≅G′G/\ker\varphi \cong G'G/kerφ≅G′. The construction is not special to groups: the same proof gives A/ker⁡f≅im⁡fA/\ker f \cong \operatorname{im} fA/kerf≅imf for any "set + operations + axioms" structure, while at the level of bare sets every map induces a bijection A/∼→im⁡fA/{\sim} \to \operatorname{im} fA/∼→imf with a∼a′  ⟺  f(a)=f(a′)a \sim a' \iff f(a) = f(a')a∼a′⟺f(a)=f(a′), and only the final step — that the quotient by the kernel is again a group — is new. The theorem separates what a homomorphism collapses (the kernel) from what survives (the image), which is why group theory is largely the study of normal subgroups.

Statement

Let φ:G→G′\varphi : G \to G'φ:G→G′ be a group homomorphism. Then φ\varphiφ induces an isomorphism

G/ker⁡φ  ≅  im⁡φ,gker⁡φ  ⟼  φ(g).G \big/ \ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi, \qquad g \ker\varphi \;\longmapsto\; \varphi(g).G/kerφ≅imφ,gkerφ⟼φ(g).

In particular: the image of a surjective homomorphism is the quotient group (G/ker⁡φ≅G′G/\ker\varphi \cong G'G/kerφ≅G′ when φ\varphiφ is surjective).

Notes

⭐ It is really a theorem about the format "set + operations + axioms", not a privilege of groups. The same proof carries over verbatim once "homomorphism" is replaced by the corresponding notion:

A/ker⁡f  ≅  im⁡f.A \big/ \ker f \;\cong\; \operatorname{im} f.A/kerf≅imf.

⭐ The set-level prototype: for any map f:A→Bf : A \to Bf:A→B, define on AAA the relation a∼a′  ⟺  f(a)=f(a′)a \sim a' \iff f(a) = f(a')a∼a′⟺f(a)=f(a′); then fff induces a bijection A/∼  →  im⁡fA/{\sim} \;\to\; \operatorname{im} fA/∼→imf (see "quotient set and equivalence classes").

The group version adds just one sentence: after quotienting by the kernel, what remains is a group.

The first two steps (well defined, bijection) are the set-level content; only the third (homomorphism) is new.

What question does it answer? "Where does a homomorphism send a group?" has a two-part answer: the kernel (what is collapsed) and the image (what survives), locked together by one isomorphism. So studying homomorphisms is studying normal subgroups — which is why group theory becomes the study of the subgroup lattice.
