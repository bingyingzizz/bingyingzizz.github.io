Statement

In K(C)\mathbf{K}(\mathcal{C})K(C):

  1. K→ 1 K→0→K[1]K \xrightarrow{\ 1\ } K \to 0 \to K[1]K 1 ​K→0→K[1] is a distinguished triangle;
  2. Rotation: K→ f L→ g M→ h K[1]K \xrightarrow{\ f\ } L \xrightarrow{\ g\ } M \xrightarrow{\ h\ } K[1]K f ​L g ​M h ​K[1] is distinguished   ⟺  \iff⟺ the rotated L→ g M→ h K[1]→ −f[1] L[1]L \xrightarrow{\ g\ } M \xrightarrow{\ h\ } K[1] \xrightarrow{\ -f[1]\ } L[1]L g ​M h ​K[1] −f[1] ​L[1] is distinguished;
  3. every f:K→Lf : K \to Lf:K→L embeds (non-uniquely) into a distinguished triangle;
  4. morphisms between distinguished triangles extend (non-uniquely).

Notes

Corollary: the mapping cone is unique in K(C)\mathbf{K}(\mathcal{C})K(C) (unique up to homotopy). Hence "the cone of fff" is a well-defined object in K(C)\mathbf{K}(\mathcal{C})K(C) — although the explicit formula for M(f)M(f)M(f) depends on the representative of fff.

The word "non-uniquely" appears twice among the four items — that is exactly the difference between derived and triangulated categories:

  • ① and ② (rotation): this is the core of the triangle axiom. Given a distinguished K→L→M→K[1]K \to L \to M \to K[1]K→L→M→K[1], rotating gives L→M→K[1]→L[1]L \to M \to K[1] \to L[1]L→M→K[1]→L[1] (the third arrow carries the sign −f[1]-f[1]−f[1]). ⚠ The sign cannot be dropped: writing f[1]f[1]f[1] would contradict what one gets by rotating twice (four rotations return to the start, so the signs must multiply to +1+1+1).
  • ③ every fff embeds in a distinguished triangle: this step needs the mapping cone (映射锥), and is non-unique — but different choices differ only by a non-unique isomorphism (not a unique one). ⚠ One cannot say "cones in K(C)\mathbf{K}(\mathcal{C})K(C) are concrete, hence unique": an explicit cone model only guarantees existence, and the isomorphism is not unique — take a field kkk, L=k[0]L = k[0]L=k[0], K=L[−1]K = L[-1]K=L[−1], f=0f = 0f=0; the cone is L⊕K[1]≅k2[0]L\oplus K[1]\cong k^{2}[0]L⊕K[1]≅k2[0] with differential 000, so for every a≠0a\ne0a=0 the matrix (1a01)\begin{pmatrix}1 & a\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}(10​a1​) is an automorphism of it, and it is not homotopic to the identity (d=0\mathrm{d} = 0d=0   ⟹  \implies⟹ any homotopy must be zero, yet this one is not). So "the cone is unique" holds only up to isomorphism class; the difference between derived and general triangulated categories lies in constructibility (there one can take cones explicitly), not in uniqueness.
  • ④ morphisms extend: also non-unique, and cannot be proved by "taking a cone" (general triangulated categories have no cone construction); one must use axiom TR3.

⚠ "Non-unique" does not mean "choose arbitrarily": the difference between extensions is controlled (a homotopy, or some degree of freedom), but there is no canonical choice. That is why one cannot work "constructively" in a triangulated category as in a derived category — extra enhanced structure (dg categories, stable ∞\infty∞-categories) is needed to turn "choice" into "property".
