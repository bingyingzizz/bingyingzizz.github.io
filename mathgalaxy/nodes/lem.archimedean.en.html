Statement

Let KKK be a complete ordered field (for example R\mathbb{R}R). Then:

(i) Archimedean property: the natural numbers are unbounded in KKK —

∀x∈K, ∃n∈N:n>x\forall x \in K,\ \exists n \in \mathbb{N} : n > x∀x∈K, ∃n∈N:n>x

(ii) Q\mathbb{Q}Q is dense in KKK: between any two elements there lies a rational —

∀x,y∈K, x<y  ⟹  ∃q∈Q:x<q<y\forall x, y \in K,\ x < y \implies \exists q \in \mathbb{Q} : x < q < y∀x,y∈K, x<y⟹∃q∈Q:x<q<y

(Here Q\mathbb{Q}Q denotes the embedded image of Q\mathbb{Q}Q in KKK: 111 generates Z\mathbb{Z}Z in KKK, and taking fractions gives Q\mathbb{Q}Q.)

Notes

⭐ Both items are consequences of completeness (the supremum principle); no further assumption is needed.

Their uses are extensive:

  • Proving the uniqueness of the complete ordered field (well-definedness of R\mathbb{R}R) relies on (ii) — the rationals are dense on both sides, which is what allows approximation of arbitrary elements by "images of rationals";
  • Every "take nnn large enough" and "take ε=1/n\varepsilon = 1/nε=1/n" in analysis is really an application of (i);
  • (ii) is also the licence for "approximating reals by rationals": every real number is a supremum of rationals.

⚠ Completeness   ⟹  \implies⟹ the Archimedean property, but not conversely: Q\mathbb{Q}Q is Archimedean yet not complete.

⚠ The qqq squeezed in (ii) must lie strictly between the two numbers — this is precisely where "dense" is stronger than "there are rationals arbitrarily close".
