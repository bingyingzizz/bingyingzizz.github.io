Statement

In plain language: A smooth family of directions fits together into sheets if and only if it is involutive. Involutivity is the only obstruction. This is the geometric version of solvability for systems of first-order partial differential equations, and it explains how foliations arise.

Let D\mathcal{D}D be a tangent distribution on an nnn-dimensional smooth manifold MMM, smooth and of rank kkk. The following four conditions are equivalent:

  1. D\mathcal{D}D is involutive (involutive distribution).
  2. Existence of flat coordinates: Each point ppp has a neighborhood UUU with coordinates (x1,…,xn)(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})(x1,…,xn) in which D∣U\mathcal{D}|_{U}D∣U​ is spanned by ∂1,…,∂k\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{k}∂1​,…,∂k​.
  3. Local integrability: Each point ppp lies in an kkk-dimensional integral manifold (integral manifold).
  4. Existence of a foliation: MMM is covered by a family of pairwise disjoint maximal connected integral manifolds (leaves), each of which is an immersed submanifold.

In particular, involutivity holds if and only if there are always local coordinates that straighten D\mathcal{D}D into coordinate slices. When k=n−1k = n-1k=n−1 and D=ker⁡ω\mathcal{D} = \ker\omegaD=kerω (with ω\omegaω nowhere zero), involutivity is equivalent to

ω∧dω=0,\omega \wedge \mathrm{d}\omega = 0,ω∧dω=0,

This is the classical Frobenius condition. Its proof is the explicit calculation for hyperplane distributions corresponding to the flow-box argument for (1)  ⟺  \iff⟺(2) below.

IV. (2)  ⟹  \implies⟹(4): Each point has a coordinate chart NqN_{q}Nq​. Order all connected integral manifolds by inclusion and apply Zorn's lemma to obtain maximal elements. The maximal elements are pairwise disjoint, since otherwise their union would still be a connected integral manifold, and they cover MMM. We need MMM to be second countable to ensure that the set of leaves is countable and the construction is well-defined. Second countability is already part of the definition of a manifold. VI. The condition is sharp. A contact distribution is a counterexample (see the notes to involutive distribution). It is smooth, has rank 222, and is nowhere zero, yet fails to be involutive, so no surface can be an integral manifold for it. Involutivity is therefore necessary as well as sufficient.

VII. The conclusion is local. The theorem gives foliation coordinates near each point. It does not guarantee global coordinates straightening D\mathcal{D}D into coordinate slices of Rn\mathbb{R}^{n}Rn, which would require the leaf space itself to be a product manifold. The irrational-slope foliation on the torus is a counterexample. Its leaves are locally straight lines, yet each winds densely around the torus.

VIII. Analytic formulation and smoothness. Frobenius gives the compatibility condition for local solvability of the system of first-order linear partial differential equations ∂if=∑jaij∂jf\partial_{i}f=\sum_{j}a^{j}_{i}\partial_{j}f∂i​f=∑j​aij​∂j​f (i>ki>ki>k). Compatibility is precisely involutivity, which is also how to read d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2}=0d2=0. Step 1 uses Picard–Lindelöf to solve the ODE, so D\mathcal{D}D must be smooth (at least C2C^{2}C2 to discuss brackets). Below C1C^{1}C1, there are counterexamples that are nowhere involutive yet have integral surfaces everywhere.

IX. Notation. Here we use the language of families of kkk-dimensional subspaces. Textbooks often use integral manifolds of a differential ideal I⊆Ω∙(M)I\subseteq\Omega^{\bullet}(M)I⊆Ω∙(M). The two formulations are equivalent (take III to be the ideal generated by forms annihilating D\mathcal{D}D). We use only the distribution formulation here to avoid introducing a second notation. The cases k=nk=nk=n and k=0k=0k=0 are trivial. k=1k=1k=1 is always involutive, and the nontrivial examples begin at 2≤k≤n−12\le k\le n-12≤k≤n−1.
