Statement

What it does: the most economical way of adding a single point — add one point ∞\infty∞ so that every sequence running off to infinity converges to it. The passage from Rn\mathbb{R}^{n}Rn to SnS^{n}Sn, and from C\mathbb{C}C to the Riemann sphere, are both instances of this construction.

Let XXX be a topological space. Take X∗:=X⊔{∞}X^{*} := X \sqcup \{\infty\}X∗:=X⊔{∞}, with the topology whose open sets are

  1. the open sets of XXX;
  2. sets of the form U∪{∞}U \cup \{\infty\}U∪{∞}, where U⊆XU \subseteq XU⊆X is open and X∖UX \setminus UX∖U is compact in XXX.

X∗X^{*}X∗ is called the one-point compactification of XXX (also the Alexandrov compactification).

Theorem:

  • X∗X^{*}X∗ is compact (for arbitrary XXX);
  • X∗X^{*}X∗ is Hausdorff   ⟺  \iff⟺ XXX is a locally compact Hausdorff space.

Notes

⭐ The topological intuition: U∪{∞}U \cup \{\infty\}U∪{∞} is open   ⟺  \iff⟺ its complement is compact. Thus "the complement of XXX is compact" is exactly "a neighbourhood of ∞\infty∞" — the space around the point at infinity is precisely "what remains after XXX runs off". Accordingly, a ∞\infty∞-neighbourhood of Rn\mathbb{R}^{n}Rn is "the outside of the ball", and SnS^{n}Sn is the north pole.

⭐ Standard examples: (Rn)∗≅Sn(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*} \cong S^{n}(Rn)∗≅Sn (stereographic projection; when n=1n = 1n=1, R∪{∞}≅S1\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\} \cong S^{1}R∪{∞}≅S1);

C∗≅S2\mathbb{C}^{*} \cong S^{2}C∗≅S2 is the Riemann sphere, on which complex analysis treats ∞\infty∞ as an ordinary point;

the one-point compactification of N\mathbb{N}N is {1/n}∪{0}\{1/n\} \cup \{0\}{1/n}∪{0}.

⚠️ Why local compactness is indispensable: when XXX is not locally compact, X∗X^{}X∗ is not Hausdorff — intuitively, "a neighbourhood of ∞\infty∞" is too large and always meets the neighbourhood of some point. So existence and Hausdorffness of the one-point compactification are two separate matters: X∗X^{}X∗ is always compact, but making it Hausdorff costs local compactness.

⚠️ Relation to imp.lch-cr: that implication proves "locally compact Hausdorff spaces are completely regular" using exactly "X∗X^{}X∗ is compact Hausdorff   ⟹  \implies⟹ normal,   ⟹  \implies⟹ a Urysohn function can be built on X∗X^{}X∗ and pulled back". The one-point compactification is the standard interface for transferring the good properties of compact spaces to XXX.

The two conclusions rest on completely different hypotheses (which is why they deserve separate statements):

  • X∗X^{}X∗ is compact: no hypothesis is needed. Take any open cover of X∗X^{}X∗; some member contains ∞\infty∞ (call it U∪{∞}U\cup\{\infty\}U∪{∞}), so X∖UX\setminus UX∖U is compact; it is covered by the remaining open sets, compactness yields a finite subcover, and adding U∪{∞}U\cup\{\infty\}U∪{∞} itself gives a finite subcover. The whole argument uses only "an open cover of a compact set has a finite subcover" — neither Hausdorffness nor local compactness is used.
  • X∗X^{*}X∗ is Hausdorff   ⟺  \iff⟺ XXX is locally compact Hausdorff: both directions cost something.

⋅\cdot⋅ (⇐\Leftarrow⇐) uses local compactness: given x∈Xx \in Xx∈X, separating it from ∞\infty∞ requires "a compact neighbourhood KKK of xxx", so X∖KX\setminus KX∖K is open and K∘K^{\circ}K∘ is an open neighbourhood of xxx, the two disjoint. Without local compactness this compact neighbourhood cannot be produced. ⋅\cdot⋅ (  ⟹  \implies⟹): deriving Hausdorffness of XXX from that of X∗X^{*}X∗ is free (subspaces inherit it); deriving local compactness uses closedness of compact sets: an open neighbourhood U∪{∞}U\cup\{\infty\}U∪{∞} of ∞\infty∞ has X∖UX\setminus UX∖U compact, so the closure of UUU in XXX … — this direction is exactly the use of "compact sets are closed in a compact Hausdorff space".

⚠️ So "the one-point compactification always exists" must be read carefully: X∗X^{*}X∗ always exists as a compact space; but it is Hausdorff only when XXX is locally compact Hausdorff. Many textbooks state only the former, so beginners conclude that "every space can be compactified" — false: those admitting a Hausdorff compactification are exactly the locally compact Hausdorff spaces.

⚠️ Why neighbourhoods of ∞\infty∞ look like that: "compact complement" in the second axiom is not arbitrary — if the neighbourhood is U∪{∞}U\cup\{\infty\}U∪{∞}, then points "running to infinity" must eventually lie in UUU, i.e. eventually leave X∖UX\setminus UX∖U; requiring X∖UX\setminus UX∖U to be compact is exactly what aligns "leaving it" with "compactness", making X∗X^{*}X∗ compact.
