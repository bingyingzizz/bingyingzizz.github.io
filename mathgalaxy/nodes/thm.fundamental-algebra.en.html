Hypotheses used in each direction (the equivalences between the forms are algebra; that complex numbers have roots is the core theorem):

  • every nonconstant polynomial has a root   ⟺  \iff⟺ the field is algebraically closed: free by unfolding the usual definition; here only univariate polynomials over a field are treated.
  • a root   ⟹  \implies⟹ factorization into linear factors: use the factor theorem and induction on degree; conversely, a factorization of a nonconstant polynomial immediately gives a root, with no analysis needed.
  • all irreducible polynomials are linear   ⟺  \iff⟺ complete splitting: use irreducible factorization in the polynomial ring over a field; uniqueness uses C[z]\mathbb C[z]C[z], roots counted with multiplicity, multiplicities positive integers, order of factors irrelevant, constants by the empty product.
  • complex polynomials really have roots: this page's proof uses compactness of the closed disk, the extreme value theorem for continuous functions, and the polar form of complex numbers to choose a direction; over R\mathbb RR, z2+1z^2+1z2+1 has no root, so the base-field hypothesis cannot be dropped.
