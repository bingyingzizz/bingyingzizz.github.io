Statement

What this does: it presents the integral over a chain as a pairing — a chain on the left, a form on the right, a number as the result, linear in each slot. This bilinearity is the licence for all later splitting and summing.

Let ccc be, on MMM, a chain of degree kkk; let ω,η\omega,\etaω,η be kkk-forms, and let a,b∈Za,b\in\mathbb{Z}a,b∈Z. Then

∫c(a ω+b η)=a∫cω+b∫cη,∫a c+b c′ω=a∫cω+b∫c′ω.\int_{c}(a\,\omega+b\,\eta) = a\int_{c}\omega+b\int_{c}\eta ,\qquad \int_{a\,c+b\,c'}\omega = a\int_{c}\omega+b\int_{c'}\omega .∫c​(aω+bη)=a∫c​ω+b∫c​η,∫ac+bc′​ω=a∫c​ω+b∫c′​ω.

In particular ∫−cω=−∫cω\int_{-c}\omega = -\int_{c}\omega∫−c​ω=−∫c​ω (a chain with coefficient −1-1−1 is "walking backwards").

Thus integration is a bilinear pairing

Ck(M)×Ωk(M)⟶R,C_{k}(M)\times\Omega^{k}(M)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},Ck​(M)×Ωk(M)⟶R,

with the geometric object (cubes and their sums) on the left and the dual object (forms and their sums) on the right.

Notes

Why this deserves its own node: it is the legal basis for every "split and compute" operation — splitting ω\omegaω into components, splitting a chain into cubes, splitting a region into small pieces all use these two linearities. Without it, Stokes' theorem could only be stated for a single cube, never "for a chain".

Pairing viewpoint: Ωk(M)\Omega^{k}(M)Ωk(M) is a vector space over the reals (infinite-dimensional), and Ck(M)C_{k}(M)Ck​(M) is a free abelian group; the two pair to R\mathbb{R}R, each linearly.

The dual directions are "d\mathrm{d}d raises degree, ∂\partial∂ lowers degree", and ∫\int∫ is precisely the bracket that couples this pair of duals (链上的 Stokes 定理).

⚠️ Antisymmetry is not "antisymmetry": ∫−c=−∫c\int_{-c} = -\int_{c}∫−c​=−∫c​ says the coefficients of the chain are negative, not that "swapping the two arguments changes the sign". There is no commutativity between chains and forms — they have different types.

⚠️ Coefficient field: the coefficients of a chain lie in Z\mathbb{Z}Z (奇异链), those of a form lie in R\mathbb{R}R. Here a,ba,ba,b are integers in Z\mathbb{Z}Z (an integral linear combination of chains is still a chain); if real coefficients are needed, one must first write the chain group as Ck(M;R)C_{k}(M;\mathbb{R})Ck​(M;R).
