Statement

Let GGG be a group.

(a) Subgroups: if GGG is solvable and H≤GH \le GH≤G, then HHH is solvable.

(b) Quotients: if GGG is solvable and N⊴GN \trianglelefteq GN⊴G, then G/NG/NG/N is solvable.

(c) Extensions: if N⊴GN \trianglelefteq GN⊴G, NNN is solvable and G/NG/NG/N is solvable, then GGG is solvable.

((a) and (b) say that solvability descends; (c) says that solvability ascends along normal extensions.)

Notes

⭐ (c) is the most useful of the three: to show that GGG is solvable it suffices to find a normal subgroup with GGG sandwiched in between and both ends solvable — the standard way of breaking a big group into small pieces.

⚠ The converses of (a) and (b) both fail, with tiny counterexamples:

  • a solvable subgroup does not imply that the group is solvable: Z/2≤A5\mathbb{Z}/2 \le A_5Z/2≤A5​ is solvable, A5A_5A5​ is not;
  • a solvable quotient does not imply that the group is solvable: S5/A5≅Z/2S_5 / A_5 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2S5​/A5​≅Z/2 is solvable, S5S_5S5​ is not.

So "solvable" is not a local property; it ascends only by squeezing between two solvable ends as in (c).

⭐ In terms of derived series all three are almost a single sentence: H(k)≤G(k)H^{(k)} \le G^{(k)}H(k)≤G(k);

π(G(k))=(G/N)(k)\pi(G^{(k)}) = (G/N)^{(k)}π(G(k))=(G/N)(k).

The full proof is on the edges.

⭐ This immediately gives a commonly used criterion: GGG is solvable   ⟺  \iff⟺ there is a normal chain whose quotients are cyclic (not merely abelian) — because an abelian group can be refined further into cyclic factors.
