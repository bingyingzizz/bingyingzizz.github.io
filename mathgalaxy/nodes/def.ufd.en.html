Statement

An integral domain AAA is a unique factorization domain (UFD) if

  1. Existence: every non-zero non-unit aaa can be written as a product of finitely many irreducible elements;
  2. Uniqueness: two such factorizations differ only by order and by associates (that is, the factors can be paired off as associates).

Notes

Z\mathbb{Z}Z and K[x]K[x]K[x] are both UFDs (the latter by K[x] 是唯一分解整环), while Z[−5]\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​] is not:

6=2⋅3=(1+−5)(1−−5),6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}),6=2⋅3=(1+−5​)(1−−5​),

the four factors are all irreducible and pairwise non-associate — so factorization is not unique.

The criterion for UFDs: UFD   ⟺  \iff⟺ (factorization exists) and (irreducible = prime). The three properties often listed side by side are not individually equivalent to UFD: ① "irreducible = prime" (相伴、不可约与素) is enough only when existence of factorization already holds (drop existence and it is weaker — the ring of all algebraic integers has no irreducible elements, the condition is vacuously true, and yet it is not a UFD); ② a gcd⁡\gcdgcd exists for any two elements in a GCD domain (gcd 整环), strictly weaker than UFD (the graph only records UFD   ⟹  \implies⟹ GCD domain, i.e. imp.ring-ufd-gcd; the converse fails, e.g. for non-discrete valuation rings); ③ absence of infinite descending factor chains is ACCP, also strictly weaker than UFD (Z[−5]\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​] is Noetherian, hence has ACCP, but is not a UFD).

The hierarchy (imp.ring-euclid-pid / imp.ring-pid-ufd): Euclidean domain   ⟹  \implies⟹ PID   ⟹  \implies⟹ UFD   ⟹  \implies⟹ GCD domain, and both arrows are irreversible (Z[−5]\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​] does not even have gcds; there exist UFDs that are not PIDs, such as K[x,y]K[x, y]K[x,y]).
