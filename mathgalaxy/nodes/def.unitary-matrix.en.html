Statement

What it does: writes "preserves lengths and angles" as a matrix condition. In the complex case it is called unitary, in the real case orthogonal — two faces of the same condition over two fields.

Let U∈Mn(C)U \in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})U∈Mn​(C). Call UUU a unitary matrix if

U∗U=I,U^{*}U = I ,U∗U=I,

⚠️ For finite order this single equation suffices: U∗U=IU^{*}U = IU∗U=I already shows that UUU is injective, and a square matrix that is injective is invertible, so U∗=U−1U^{*} = U^{-1}U∗=U−1; right-multiplying by UUU gives UU∗=IUU^{*} = IUU∗=I (the same argument with UU∗=IUU^{*} = IUU∗=I). Equivalently U−1=U∗U^{-1} = U^{*}U−1=U∗. A real square matrix Q∈Mn(R)Q \in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})Q∈Mn​(R) satisfying QTQ=IQ^{T}Q = IQTQ=I is called an orthogonal matrix.

Equivalent characterizations: the following three are equivalent — (i) UUU is unitary; (ii) the columns of UUU form an orthonormal basis; (iii) for all x,yx, yx,y, ⟨Ux,Uy⟩=⟨x,y⟩\langle Ux, Uy\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle⟨Ux,Uy⟩=⟨x,y⟩.

Thus a unitary matrix preserves the inner product, hence the norm (∥Ux∥=∥x∥\lVert Ux\rVert = \lVert x\rVert∥Ux∥=∥x∥) and distances; ∣det⁡U∣=1\lvert\det U\rvert = 1∣detU∣=1. All unitary matrices form a group U(n)U(n)U(n), and the orthogonal ones form O(n)O(n)O(n).

Notes

The name "unitary": U∗U=IU^{*}U = IU∗U=I degenerates to orthogonal in the real case (which is what ⊥\perp⊥ means); in the complex case the term is unitary (from Latin unus, "one"). The terminology does not matter; the condition is the same.

Why it suffices that the columns form an orthonormal basis: for U∗U=IU^{*}U = IU∗U=I, its (j,k)(j,k)(j,k)-th entry is exactly "the inner product of the jjj-th column with the kkk-th column = δjk\delta_{jk}δjk​". So "unitary" = "sends an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis", which is the most convenient criterion.

Relation to def.isometry: unitary matrices are exactly the matrices of "inner-product-preserving linear maps in an orthonormal basis". ⚠️ Inner-product-preserving operators (isometries) and unitary matrices correspond one-to-one only in an orthonormal basis; after a change of basis the matrix is no longer unitary, but the operator still preserves the inner product.

⭐ Numerical significance: unitary transformations do not amplify errors (∥Ux∥=∥x∥\lVert Ux\rVert = \lVert x\rVert∥Ux∥=∥x∥), so "unitary similarity" is the most stable kind of similarity — this is why both Schur triangularization and the SVD choose unitary transformations.
