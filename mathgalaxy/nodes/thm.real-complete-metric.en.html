Statement

On R\mathbb{R}R take the distance d(x,y):=∣x−y∣d(x, y) := |x - y|d(x,y):=∣x−y∣. Then (R,d)(\mathbb{R}, d)(R,d) is a complete metric space: every Cauchy sequence in it converges.

This is exactly the Cauchy criterion rewritten in the language of metric spaces — in one sentence: R\mathbb{R}R is complete.

Notes

⭐ This is the interface of two lines: on one side "R\mathbb{R}R is a complete ordered field" (supremum principle), on the other "a metric space XXX is complete" (every Cauchy sequence converges).

On R\mathbb{R}R the two coincide, but they come from different sources: the first is an order property, the second a metric property.

⭐ Following this edge outward leads to completion: every metric space embeds into a complete metric space (take Cauchy sequences and quotient), and the construction of R\mathbb{R}R is the prototype — completing Q\mathbb{Q}Q gives R\mathbb{R}R.

⚠ In a general metric space "complete" and "compact" are different: R\mathbb{R}R is complete but not compact.
