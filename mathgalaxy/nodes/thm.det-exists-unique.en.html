Statement

Let dim⁡KV=n\dim_K V = ndimK​V=n. Then there is a unique function

D:Vn⟶KD : V^{n} \longrightarrow KD:Vn⟶K

satisfying:

(i) Multilinear: linear in each argument separately;

(ii) Alternating: it takes the value 000 whenever two arguments are equal;

(iii) Normalized: D(e1,…,en)=1D(e_1, \ldots, e_n) = 1D(e1​,…,en​)=1 ((ei)(e_i)(ei​) being any fixed ordered basis).

Notes

⭐ This theorem is the correct way in which the determinant is born: not "multiply diagonals and subtract", but "the unique function satisfying three axioms".

The three axioms are linearity, alternation, normalization, and none can be dropped.

⭐ "Uniqueness" is far more useful than "existence": any function satisfying the three axioms equals the determinant, so to verify an identity it suffices to verify the three axioms — e.g. det⁡(AT)=det⁡A\det(A^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det Adet(AT)=detA, det⁡(AB)=det⁡Adet⁡B\det(AB) = \det A \det Bdet(AB)=detAdetB.

⭐ Construction for "existence": expand (v1,…,vn)(v_1, \ldots, v_n)(v1​,…,vn​) in the basis, obtaining the coefficient matrix (aij)(a_{ij})(aij​) of size n×nn \times nn×n, then take the Leibniz formula ∑σsgn⁡(σ)∏iai,σ(i)\sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_i a_{i,\sigma(i)}∑σ​sgn(σ)∏i​ai,σ(i)​ as the definition — then verify the three axioms one by one (see the proof section).

⭐ Relation to "universal property": this is not a universal property in the usual sense (there is no loose adjoint), but it is the same technique — pinning an object down by a universal condition (the three axioms).
