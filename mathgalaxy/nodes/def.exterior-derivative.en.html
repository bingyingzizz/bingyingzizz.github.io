Statement

What this does: it equips forms with a derivative that sends kkk-forms to (k+1)(k+1)(k+1)-forms and satisfies d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2} = 0d2=0 — this is what writes calculus in a coordinate-free way.

Let ω=∑IaI dxI\omega = \sum_{I}a_{I}\,\mathrm{d}x^{I}ω=∑I​aI​dxI be a kkk-form on UUU, where dxI:=dxi1∧⋯∧dxik\mathrm{d}x^{I} := \mathrm{d}x^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{i_{k}}dxI:=dxi1​∧⋯∧dxik​. The exterior derivative is defined by

dω:=∑IdaI∧dxI=∑I∑j=1n∂aI∂xj dxj∧dxI.\mathrm{d}\omega := \sum_{I}\mathrm{d}a_{I}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{I} = \sum_{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial a_{I}}{\partial x^{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x^{j}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{I}.dω:=I∑​daI​∧dxI=I∑​j=1∑n​∂xj∂aI​​dxj∧dxI.

For a function (a 000-form) it is the total differential df=∑j∂jf dxj\mathrm{d}f = \sum_{j}\partial_{j}f\,\mathrm{d}x^{j}df=∑j​∂j​fdxj. Basic properties:

  1. R\mathbb{R}R-linearity: d(aω+bη)=a dω+b dη\mathrm{d}(a\omega+b\eta) = a\,\mathrm{d}\omega+b\,\mathrm{d}\etad(aω+bη)=adω+bdη;
  2. Graded Leibniz rule: when ω\omegaω is a kkk-form, d(ω∧η)=dω∧η+(−1)kω∧dη\mathrm{d}(\omega\wedge\eta) = \mathrm{d}\omega\wedge\eta+(-1)^{k}\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\etad(ω∧η)=dω∧η+(−1)kω∧dη;
  3. d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2} = 0d2=0: a direct consequence of the equality of mixed partial derivatives;
  4. it is independent of the choice of coordinates (equivalently, it commutes with pullback; see 拉回（开集间）).

A form with dω=0\mathrm{d}\omega = 0dω=0 is called closed, and a form ω\omegaω with ω=dη\omega = \mathrm{d}\etaω=dη is called exact.

By d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2}=0d2=0, every exact form is closed;

the converse fails (x dy−y dxx2+y2\frac{x\,\mathrm{d}y-y\,\mathrm{d}x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}x2+y2xdy−ydx​ on R2∖{0}\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}R2∖{0} is closed but not exact).

Notes

What kind of algebraic object d\mathrm{d}d is: an antiderivation of the exterior algebra — degree +1+1+1, graded Leibniz, square zero. These three properties determine it uniquely, so there is no need to memorize the formula; remember these three.

d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2}=0d2=0 has the same origin as the structure equations: the structure equations of a surface are also a reading of d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2}=0d2=0 (结构方程). The advantage of the language of forms is that "mixed partials commute" and "the frame is integrable" become the same identity.

Unification of three classical operators (n=3n=3n=3): d\mathrm{d}d on 000-forms gives the gradient (as a 111-form), on 111-forms the curl (as a 222-form), and on 222-forms the divergence (as a 333-form). Hence d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2}=0d2=0 is exactly "the divergence of a curl is zero" and "the curl of a gradient is zero".

⚠️ One cannot apply d\mathrm{d}d to a vector field: d\mathrm{d}d acts only on forms. Differentiating vector fields requires extra structure (a connection, 仿射联络), and the result is a covariant derivative rather than an exterior derivative.

⚠️ The same symbol d\mathrm{d}d is also used for the total differential, the arc-length element and the integration measure — a historical burden of notation. In this map dω\mathrm{d}\omegadω always denotes the exterior derivative, while ds\mathrm{d}sds and dA\mathrm{d}AdA have their own meanings (弧长, 面积元).

Among the three properties, d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2} = 0d2=0 is the only one with content (the other two almost just say "it is a derivation"):

  • R\mathbb{R}R-linearity and the graded Leibniz rule only determine the action of d\mathrm{d}d on a basis. What really pins it down is the convention d(dxj)=0\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}x^{j}) = 0d(dxj)=0 — with it, d\mathrm{d}d on dxI\mathrm{d}x^{I}dxI is completely determined by the Leibniz rule.
  • d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2} = 0d2=0: this is where all the content lies; it is equivalent to the equality of mixed partials (∂j∂k=∂k∂j\partial_{j}\partial_{k} = \partial_{k}\partial_{j}∂j​∂k​=∂k​∂j​, Clairaut's theorem). ⚠️ Hence the exterior derivative is well behaved only in the C2C^{2}C2 class: functions that are C1C^{1}C1 but whose second derivatives do not commute break it.
  • Relation to the Lie derivative, d=\mathrm{d} = d= "antisymmetrization": the Cartan formula LX=dιX+ιXd\mathcal{L}_{X} = \mathrm{d}\iota_{X} + \iota_{X}\mathrm{d}LX​=dιX​+ιX​d welds these three operations together (Cartan 公式).

⚠️ Three equivalent definitions, at different costs: the coordinate formula (this section; most concrete but coordinate-dependent), the axiomatic one (Leibniz + d2=0\mathrm{d}^{2}=0d2=0 + agreement on functions; one must prove existence and uniqueness), and the coordinate-free form [ω](X0,…,Xk)=∑(−1)iXiω(…)[\omega](X_{0},\ldots,X_{k}) = \sum(-1)^{i}X_{i}\omega(\ldots)[ω](X0​,…,Xk​)=∑(−1)iXi​ω(…) (most geometric, it displays intrinsicness directly). They are equivalent, but only the last one shows that d\mathrm{d}d is independent of coordinates.

⚠️ d\mathrm{d}d is not tensorial: it does not satisfy d(fω)=f dω\mathrm{d}(f\omega) = f\,\mathrm{d}\omegad(fω)=fdω (the difference is a term df∧ω\mathrm{d}f\wedge\omegadf∧ω). This is exactly why it cannot be defined on general tensor fields, only on forms.

Hypotheses used in each direction (the coordinate formula and the axiomatic characterization must specify the action on functions):

  • coordinate formula   ⟹  \implies⟹ total differential on functions and the graded Leibniz rule: uses the product rule for partial derivatives and the sign rule for the wedge product.
  • coordinate formula   ⟹  \implies⟹ d2=0\mathrm d^2=0d2=0: requires equality of mixed partials; coefficients in C2C^2C2 suffice, while a single exterior derivative itself only needs C1C^1C1.
  • axiomatic characterization   ⟹  \implies⟹ coordinate formula: one must also require agreement with the total differential on functions; otherwise the zero operator satisfies linearity, graded Leibniz and square zero, and cannot determine the exterior derivative uniquely.
  • exact   ⟹  \implies⟹ closed uses square zero; closed   ⟹  \implies⟹ exact needs an extra local or global condition, such as the Poincaré lemma on star-shaped open sets; the angle form on the punctured plane gives a global counterexample.
