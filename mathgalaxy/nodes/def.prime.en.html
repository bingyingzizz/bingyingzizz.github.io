Statement

For 111, an integer ppp greater than it is called prime if its only positive divisors are 111 and ppp itself. An integer greater than 111 that is not prime is called composite.

Notes

The property actually used in proofs is not "has only two divisors" but Euclid's lemma: p∣ab  ⟹  p∣ap \mid ab \implies p \mid ap∣ab⟹p∣a or p∣bp \mid bp∣b.

These two are equivalent in Z\mathbb{Z}Z, but not in a general integral domain: in Z[−5]\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​], 2∣(1+−5)(1−−5)=62 \mid (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = 62∣(1+−5​)(1−−5​)=6 while 222 divides neither factor — the element is "irreducible" but not "prime". Ring theory abstracts the latter as a prime element, the former as an irreducible element.

111 is not prime: otherwise unique factorization would fail (6=2⋅3=1⋅2⋅36 = 2\cdot 3 = 1\cdot 2\cdot 36=2⋅3=1⋅2⋅3).
