Statement

Every compact surface (a compact, connected, 222-dimensional topological manifold, possibly with boundary) is triangulable (triangulation of a space).

A common equivalent formulation is that every compact connected surface is homeomorphic to the geometric realization of a finite simplicial complex.

Notes

⚠ This node retains status: skeleton. There are two common approaches to Radó's theorem, and neither is completed here. The outline below identifies the specific lemmas still needed and corrects two inaccuracies in the previous notes.

Theorem. Every compact surface is triangulable (equivalently, a compact topological 222-manifold admits a finite simplicial triangulation). An orientable compact surface also admits a compatible complex structure. See the comparison by dimension below for the precise scope of the equivalent formulation.

Surfaces with boundary. The statement allows boundary, but both approaches below address only closed surfaces. The extension requires an argument. The standard method is doubling along the boundary. Take two copies of SSS and glue them along ∂S\partial S∂S by the identity to obtain a closed surface D(S)D(S)D(S). Triangulate D(S)D(S)D(S) by either approach below, then restrict to SSS, provided the triangulation places ∂S\partial S∂S in the 111-skeleton (see gap (vi)). One can also collapse each boundary circle to a point to obtain a closed surface.

Approach A (complex analysis). An orientable compact surface →\rightarrow→ a complex structure (a Riemann surface) →\rightarrow→ existence of a nonconstant meromorphic function →\rightarrow→ the meromorphic function is a branched covering of CP1\mathbb{CP}^{1}CP1 →\rightarrow→ pull back and subdivide a triangulation of CP1\mathbb{CP}^{1}CP1 (use the branch values as vertices and subdivide into sectors near the branch points using the local model z↦zmz\mapsto z^{m}z↦zm) →\rightarrow→ obtain a finite triangulation. Gaps: (i) existence of a complex structure (uniformization or the surface case of Riemann–Roch), (ii) existence of a meromorphic function (Riemann–Roch, or Runge approximation +++ Mittag-Leffler), and (iii) subdivision and compatibility for the pullback under the branched covering. Note: The branched covering z↦zmz\mapsto z^{m}z↦zm here is distinct from the orientation double cover used below in the nonorientable case.

Approach B (differential topology). Existence of a smooth structure (Whitehead: a C1C^{1}C1 structure suffices, or construct C∞C^{\infty}C∞ directly) →\rightarrow→ a Morse function →\rightarrow→ a handle decomposition →\rightarrow→ each handle is a 0/1/20/1/20/1/2-handle, giving a CW decomposition in attachment order →\rightarrow→ triangulate each handle and subdivide compatibly →\rightarrow→ obtain a triangulation. Gaps: (iv) existence of a smooth structure, (v) existence of a Morse function (requiring C2C^{2}C2 approximation), and (vi) compatibility between the handle decomposition and triangulation (treatment of 111-handles and gluing between handles).

Can the nonorientable case use the orientation double cover? Yes, but an additional argument is needed. Let p:S~→Sp:\widetilde{S}\to Sp:S→S be the orientation double cover. On SSS there is a corresponding free Z/2\mathbb{Z}/2Z/2-action, with no fixed points and two points per fiber. Even given a triangulation of S~\widetilde{S}S, descending it to SSS requires an equivariant triangulation: there must be a triangulation of S~\widetilde{S}S for which the involution is a simplicial automorphism. The equivariant triangulation lemma gives this using two barycentric subdivisions. The first makes the involution map simplices to simplices, and the second places the barycenters in the interiors of the simplices and makes them vertices, at which point the involution acts simplicially on the barycentric subdivision (see Illman for a more general theorem). The resulting quotient complex S~/(Z/2)\widetilde{S}/(\mathbb{Z}/2)S/(Z/2) is a triangulation of SSS. The quotient map is bijective on each open simplex, and the simplicial automorphism determines the gluings. The previous notes said to use the local model z↦z2z\mapsto z^{2}z↦z2 at branch points. This is incorrect. The orientation double cover has no branch points because the action is free. The model z↦z2z\mapsto z^{2}z↦z2 applies to the branched covering from a meromorphic function in Approach A. Confusing the two can make the quotient construction seem immediate.

Why this theorem concerns dimension 2. dim⁡≤2\dim\le 2dim≤2: Radó (1925). dim⁡=3\dim=3dim=3: Moise (1952). dim⁡=4\dim=4dim=4: Freedman (1982, topological manifolds admit triangulations, with E8E_{8}E8​ counterexamples in the smooth/PL case in the same dimension). dim⁡≥5\dim\ge 5dim≥5: Kirby–Siebenmann (1969), who showed that some compact topological manifolds are not triangulable. Thus triangulability of all compact manifolds holds only in low dimensions, and the surface theorem does not follow from a general result of this form.

Two distinctions. (a) Radó's existence theorem and the claim that any two triangulations are combinatorially equivalent are distinct. The latter is the Hauptvermutung (false in dim⁡≥4\dim\ge 4dim≥4, true in dim⁡=2\dim=2dim=2) and is the work of step (2) of classification of closed surfaces. (b) Existence of a complex structure and existence of a smooth structure are equivalent in dim⁡=2\dim=2dim=2 (uniformization) and inequivalent in dim⁡≥4\dim\ge 4dim≥4.

Remaining gaps. (i) Existence of a complex structure (uniformization or Riemann–Roch), (ii) existence of a meromorphic function, (iii) subdivision of a pullback under a branched covering, (iv) an equivariant triangulation lemma for a free involution, (v) existence of a smooth structure +++ Morse +++ handle decomposition (Approach B), and (vi) the boundary case: doubling gives a closed surface, and the triangulation can be chosen to put the boundary in the 111-skeleton (equivariance under the doubling involution). The graph has no nodes for these steps, so this node remains a skeleton. The first two suggested additions are equivariant triangulation (two barycentric subdivisions), which would also cover the nonorientable case, and the uniformization theorem, on which Approach A depends.
