Statement

What it does: in general topology sequences do not suffice — neither compactness nor continuity can be characterised by sequences (unless the space is first countable). Replacing N\mathbb{N}N by an arbitrary directed set yields a “net”, and then every theorem about sequences carries over.

Directed set: a preordered set (Λ,≤)(\Lambda, \le)(Λ,≤) such that any λ,μ∈Λ\lambda, \mu \in \Lambdaλ,μ∈Λ have an upper bound (there exists ν\nuν with λ,μ≤ν\lambda, \mu \le \nuλ,μ≤ν).

Net: a map x:Λ→Xx : \Lambda \to Xx:Λ→X, written (xλ)λ∈Λ(x_{\lambda})_{\lambda \in \Lambda}(xλ​)λ∈Λ​.

Convergence: a net xλx_{\lambda}xλ​ converges to xxx (written xλ→xx_{\lambda} \to xxλ​→x) if for every neighbourhood UUU of xxx there exists λ0\lambda_{0}λ0​ such that

λ≥λ0  ⟹  xλ∈U.\lambda \ge \lambda_{0} \implies x_{\lambda} \in U .λ≥λ0​⟹xλ​∈U.

Two characterisation theorems:

  • f:X→Yf : X \to Yf:X→Y is continuous   ⟺  \iff⟺ fff sends every convergent net to a convergent net (连续映射);
  • XXX is compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ every net in XXX has a convergent subnet.

Notes

⚠️ Why sequences are not enough: in an uncountable space a singleton can be closed without being “sequentially closed” (for example ω1\omega_{1}ω1​ in [0,ω1][0,\omega_{1}][0,ω1​]: no sequence approaches it from below, yet it lies in the closure).

So “x∈A‾x \in \overline{A}x∈A   ⟺  \iff⟺ there is a sequence in AAA converging to xxx” holds only in first-countable spaces.

Nets fix this at once by replacing N\mathbb{N}N with “the reverse order of the neighbourhoods of xxx”.

⭐ Subnet ≠\ne= subsequence: a subnet may use a coarser index set and is therefore far more flexible than a subsequence (a subsequence requires an order-preserving embedding N→N\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}N→N, which misses many cases). Hence the net characterisation of compactness must use subnets; using “subsequences” is wrong.

⭐ Nets come from Riemann sums: Moore–Smith (1922) invented nets precisely to give a rigorous meaning to “∫f\int f∫f is the limit of Riemann sums” — the index set of those sums is the refinement order of partitions, not N\mathbb{N}N. So “nets” are not abstract fussiness; they solve a real problem in analysis.

⚠ Nets and filters are equivalent languages (eq.net-filter): each net corresponds to a filter, with matching convergence. Which one to use is a matter of habit — analysis books prefer nets, set theory and topology books prefer filters.

Which hypothesis each direction uses (the definition of a net is free; the two characterisations need general-topology arguments):

  • Net convergence   ⟺  \iff⟺ eventually lies in every neighbourhood: definition unpacking; the index directed set is by convention nonempty and need not be antisymmetric.
  • Continuity   ⟹  \implies⟹ preserves net limits: only the preimage-of-neighbourhood definition is used; conversely, at a discontinuity one constructs a net by choosing counterexample points in neighbourhoods, a standard construction that uses choice and needs neither first countability nor Hausdorffness.
  • Compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ every net has a convergent subnet: the duality between cluster points of nets and finite subcovers of open covers; Hausdorffness is not needed, but the subnet must satisfy the standard cofinality condition.
  • Replacing nets by sequences cannot characterise general compactness: the first-countable ordinal space [0,ω1)[0,\omega_1)[0,ω1​) is sequentially compact but not compact, so the first countability mentioned in the parenthetical is not enough to guarantee the sequential characterisation of compactness.
