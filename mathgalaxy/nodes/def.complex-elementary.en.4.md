⭐ **Euler's formula** $e^{iy} = \cos y + i\sin y$ is a direct product of these definitions: $e^{z} = e^{x}e^{iy}$ separates "modulus $e^{x}$" from "argument $y$".

⚠ **Differences from the real case**: $|e^{z}| = e^{x}$ depends only on the real part, so $e^{z}$ **never takes** $\mathbb{C}$ as a value on $0$; $\sin z$ and $\cos z$ are **unbounded** on the complex plane ($|\sin(iy)| \to \infty$), which does not conflict with $|\sin| \le 1$ on the real axis — boundedness on the real axis is not a general property of holomorphic functions.
