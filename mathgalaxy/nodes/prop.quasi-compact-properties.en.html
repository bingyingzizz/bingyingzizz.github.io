Statement

  1. In a site C\mathcal{C}C, XXX is quasi-compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ XXX is quasi-compact in the standard topology;
  2. If XXX has a finite cover by quasi-compact objects, then XXX is quasi-compact;
  3. On a pretopos C\mathcal{C}C (with the precanonical topology), a sheaf FFF is quasi-compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ there is a surjection X→FX \to FX→F with X∈CX \in \mathcal{C}X∈C.

∎

Item 3 is the most useful: it replaces "the sheaf FFF is quasi-compact" by "FFF can be covered by a small object" — in condensed sets, this is "a condensed set XXX is quasi-compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ there is a surjection from a compact Hausdorff space onto it".

Assumptions used in each direction (the finite-cover criterion relies on the correspondence between site covers and sheaf covers):

  • Item 1 needs, in both directions, the comparison of covers between the site and its sheaf category, reading representable objects as sheafified representable presheaves; only in the subcanonical case may one omit sheafification. This is a comparison of covers, not something provable by renaming.
  • Item 2 derives quasi-compactness from a finite quasi-compact cover: pull an arbitrary cover back to each piece, take finite subfamilies, then use transitivity of covers; the one-point cover of an infinite discrete space shows that "finite" cannot be dropped.
  • Item 3, forward: representable sheaves generate the sheaf category, giving a surjective cover; quasi-compactness extracts finitely many branches, and finite coproducts combine them into a single XXX; the last step uses preservation of finite coproducts under the precanonical topology.
  • Item 3, backward: when hX→Fh_X\to FhX​→F is surjective and hXh_XhX​ is quasi-compact, pull the cover back, take a finite subfamily, and descend the cover along surjectivity. The key point is that representable sheaves are quasi-compact for this topology; this cannot be assumed for free on an arbitrary site, e.g. the terminal sheaf of a non-quasi-compact topological space.
