Statement

Let B=(v1,…,vn)\mathcal{B} = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)B=(v1​,…,vn​) be an ordered basis of VVV (the order is part of the data). By the definition of a basis, every v∈Vv \in Vv∈V can be written uniquely as

v=c1v1+⋯+cnvn,ci∈Kv = c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_n v_n, \qquad c_i \in Kv=c1​v1​+⋯+cn​vn​,ci​∈K

Arrange the coefficients into a column

[v]B:=(c1⋮cn)∈Kn[v]_{\mathcal{B}} := \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{pmatrix} \in K^{n}[v]B​:=​c1​⋮cn​​​∈Kn

called the coordinate vector of vvv in the basis B\mathcal{B}B. Thus

v↦[v]Bv \mapsto [v]_{\mathcal{B}}v↦[v]B​

is a bijection V→KnV \to K^{n}V→Kn, and it is linear (it preserves addition and scalar multiplication).

Notes

⭐ This entry translates "an abstract vector space" into "column vectors in KnK^{n}Kn" — this is the entire justification of matrix theory: from now on, matrices are just what this looks like with respect to two different bases.

⚠ "Uniqueness" comes from the definition of a basis (both linearly independent and spanning);

if either property fails, coordinates cease to be unique (without independence there are repetitions; without spanning some vectors cannot be written).

⚠ "Ordered" cannot be dropped: replacing (v1,v2)(v_1, v_2)(v1​,v2​) by (v2,v1)(v_2, v_1)(v2​,v1​) swaps the two components of the coordinate vector — this is exactly where the permutations in the change-of-basis formula come from.
