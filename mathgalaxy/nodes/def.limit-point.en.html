Statement

Let S⊆RS \subseteq \mathbb{R}S⊆R and x∈Rx \in \mathbb{R}x∈R. A point xxx is a limit point (also called an accumulation point) of SSS iff every punctured neighbourhood of xxx meets SSS:

∀ε>0, ∃s∈S: 0<∣s−x∣<ε\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists s \in S :\ 0 < |s - x| < \varepsilon∀ε>0, ∃s∈S: 0<∣s−x∣<ε

Equivalent sequential formulation: there is a sequence S∖{x}S \setminus \{x\}S∖{x} with sn→xs_n \to xsn​→x.

Replacing SSS by the set of terms of a sequence gives "xxx is a limit point of {xn}\{x_n\}{xn​}", which means "{xn}\{x_n\}{xn​} has a subsequence converging to xxx".

Notes

⭐ "Every bounded sequence has a limit point" is the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem — one of the six faces of the completeness of the real numbers.

⚠ A limit point need not belong to SSS: the set of all rationals Q\mathbb{Q}Q has limit-point set all of R\mathbb{R}R (this is precisely what incompleteness of Q\mathbb{Q}Q looks like).

⚠ Do not confuse it with the supremum: sup⁡S\sup SsupS is about "upper bounds of SSS", while a limit point is about "which points the elements of SSS approach arbitrarily closely";

sup⁡{1,1/2,1/3,…}=1\sup\{1, 1/2, 1/3, \ldots\} = 1sup{1,1/2,1/3,…}=1 is also the unique limit point of that set, whereas sup⁡{1}=1\sup\{1\} = 1sup{1}=1 is not a limit point.
