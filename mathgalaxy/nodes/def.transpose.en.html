Statement

What it does: flips a matrix across its main diagonal. It looks like a mere notational operation, but it is exactly what the map induced on the dual space looks like in the language of matrices.

Let A=(aij)∈Mm×n(K)A = (a_{ij}) \in M_{m\times n}(K)A=(aij​)∈Mm×n​(K). The transpose AT∈Mn×m(K)A^{T} \in M_{n\times m}(K)AT∈Mn×m​(K) is defined by (AT)ij:=aji(A^{T})_{ij} := a_{ji}(AT)ij​:=aji​.

Basic properties: (A+B)T=AT+BT(A+B)^{T} = A^{T} + B^{T}(A+B)T=AT+BT, (cA)T=cAT(cA)^{T} = cA^{T}(cA)T=cAT, (AT)T=A(A^{T})^{T} = A(AT)T=A, and most importantly

(AB)T=BTAT(AB)^{T} = B^{T} A^{T}(AB)T=BTAT

(the order of multiplication reverses.) If AAA is invertible, then ATA^{T}AT is invertible and (A−1)T=(AT)−1(A^{-1})^{T} = (A^{T})^{-1}(A−1)T=(AT)−1.

Notes

Why the order of multiplication reverses: the transpose is not "a rearrangement"; it is duality.

A linear map T:V→WT : V \to WT:V→W induces the dual map T∗:W∗→V∗T^{*} : W^{*} \to V^{*}T∗:W∗→V∗ (with the arrow reversed), and the matrix of T∗T^{*}T∗ is exactly the transpose of the matrix of TTT.

Once the direction is reversed, the order of composition must reverse as well — this is what (AB)T=BTAT(AB)^{T} = B^{T}A^{T}(AB)T=BTAT says.

Compatibility with the pairing: regarding vectors as columns, yTAxy^{T}AxyTAx is a scalar, and equals (ATy)Tx(A^{T}y)^{T}x(ATy)Tx — moving AAA from one side to the other rests entirely on this, and it is the starting point of everything about adjoint operators and symmetric matrices that follows.

⚠️ The transpose depends on the choice of basis (it uses the dual basis), whereas the conjugate transpose (A∗A^{*}A∗) of the next section equals "the matrix of the adjoint operator" only in an orthonormal basis. Confusing the two is the most common accident for beginners.

⚠️ Do not remember (AB)T=BTAT(AB)^{T} = B^{T}A^{T}(AB)T=BTAT as ATBTA^{T}B^{T}ATBT: the latter can hold only when A,BA, BA,B are both square and happen to commute, and in general it fails.
