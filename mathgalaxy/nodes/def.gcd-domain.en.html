Statement

An integral domain AAA is called a gcd domain** if every a,b∈Aa, b \in Aa,b∈A has a greatest common divisor**: there exists ddd such that

d∣a,d∣b,d \mid a,\quad d \mid b,d∣a,d∣b,

and every ccc dividing both a,ba, ba,b divides ddd.

Note that "greatest" means the **greatest lower bound in the divisibility order**, not comparison of size — an integral domain has no total order.

Notes

Why it must be defined this way: in Z\mathbb{Z}Z the "greatest common divisor" relies on comparing ∣d∣|d|∣d∣, and a general integral domain has no absolute value. The right replacement is "divisible by every common divisor": in Z\mathbb{Z}Z it is equivalent to "largest absolute value", and in Z[i]\mathbb{Z}[i]Z[i] too this is the only statement that holds.

Hierarchy: UFD   ⟹  \implies⟹ gcd domain (imp.ring-ufd-gcd), but not conversely (Z[−5]\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​] does not even have gcd⁡(6,2(1+−5))\gcd(6, 2(1+\sqrt{-5}))gcd(6,2(1+−5​))). Bézout domain   ⟹  \implies⟹ gcd domain, because gcd⁡\gcdgcd is given directly by a linear combination.

gcd⁡\gcdgcd may fail to exist — this is exactly why "uniqueness of factorization in a UFD" cannot be replaced by gcd.
