Abstract

In a metric space the following are equivalent: compact, sequentially compact, and complete together with totally bounded. The skeleton lives in the implications: compact implies sequentially compact; sequentially compact implies totally bounded (one of two places dependent choice is used; the other is (b) implies (a)) and complete; sequentially compact implies compact by the "bad ball" argument; complete and totally bounded implies sequentially compact by diagonalisation. In general topological spaces both implications fail — sequential compactness is a metric-space luxury (counterexamples: {0,1}I\{0,1\}^{I}{0,1}I and [0,ω1)[0,\omega_1)[0,ω1​)).

Statement

Let XXX be a metric space with metric ddd. Then the following three conditions are equivalent:

  • (a) XXX is compact: every open cover has a finite subcover;
  • (b) XXX is sequentially compact: every sequence has a subsequence converging in XXX;
  • (c) XXX is complete and totally bounded.

That is, XXX compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ XXX sequentially compact   ⟺  \iff⟺ XXX complete +++ totally bounded.

Notes

The whole logical skeleton is in the arrows (click an arrow to see its proof):

  • compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ sequentially compact
  • sequentially compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ totally bounded　(one of two steps in this cluster that genuinely uses a choice principle — DC; the other is (b) implies (a))
  • sequentially compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ complete
  • sequentially compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ compact　(the "bad balls" argument; it uses total boundedness and completeness, and the bad-ball construction invokes DC)
  • compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ complete, compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ totally bounded
  • complete + totally bounded   ⟹  \implies⟹ sequentially compact　(the diagonal argument; again no choice)

So "sequentially compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ totally bounded   ⟹  \implies⟹ compact" and "compact   ⟹  \implies⟹ sequentially compact" close the circle, and the three notions agree.

⚠ What each of the two conditions does: completeness keeps the limit in the space, total boundedness supplies a shrinkable subsequence — neither can be dropped (R\mathbb{R}R is complete but not totally bounded; (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) is totally bounded but not complete).

What each direction uses, and whether any extra hypothesis is needed (this list is the content of the equivalence):

  • (a)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (b): no choice needed. If no subsequence converged, every yyy would have an open neighbourhood UyU_{y}Uy​ containing xnx_{n}xn​ for only finitely many nnn — otherwise the balls of radius 1/k1/k1/k around yyy would give a subsequence converging to yyy, with the indices picked greedily from N\mathbb{N}N. Compactness makes finitely many UyU_{y}Uy​ cover the space, so only finitely many indices occur — contradiction.
  • (a)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (c), "complete": no choice needed. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges, and to the same point.
  • (a)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (c), "totally bounded": no choice needed. Cover by all balls of radius ε\varepsilonε; a finite subcover is a finite ε\varepsilonε-net.
  • (b)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (a): uses total boundedness and completeness; the bad-ball construction invokes DC (the "bad ball" argument: if no finite cover existed, one would build recursively a sequence with pairwise distances ≥ε\ge \varepsilon≥ε, which has no convergent subsequence).
  • (b)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (c), "complete": no choice needed (the limit of the convergent subsequence is the limit of the Cauchy sequence).
  • (b)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (c), "totally bounded": this step really uses countable choice (DC) — one of two places in the proof (the other is (b) implies (a)). For each nnn one picks a 1/n1/n1/n-ball containing infinitely many terms, and shrinks layer by layer.
  • (c)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (b): no choice needed (diagonal argument: using 1/n1/n1/n-nets, keep at each layer a ball with infinitely many terms, take the diagonal subsequence — it is Cauchy — and converge by completeness).
