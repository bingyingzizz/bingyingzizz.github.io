Statement

What this does: it puts an inner product on each tangent space and requires it to vary smoothly with the point — with this, a manifold acquires lengths, angles and volumes, and hence geodesics and curvature.

Let MMM be an nnn-dimensional smooth manifold. A Riemannian metric ggg assigns to each p∈Mp\in Mp∈M an inner product

Notes

Existence: locally a metric always exists (take δij\delta_{ij}δij​ in coordinates), and then a partition of unity convexly combines the local metrics into a global one — positive definiteness is closed under convex combinations. Hence every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric (complex and symplectic structures have topological obstructions; metrics do not).

It is not part of the manifold: the same smooth manifold carries infinitely many metrics. The choice of metric is extra structure; quantities depending only on ggg are called intrinsic, those depending on an embedding extrinsic.

The two-dimensional case is the first fundamental form: restricting the inner product of R3\mathbb{R}^{3}R3 to TpST_{p}STp​S on a surface gives III. So Riemannian geometry is the higher-dimensional version of intrinsic surface geometry, and surface theory is its two-dimensional model.

The metric determines everything else: ggg uniquely determines the Levi-Civita connection and the Christoffel symbols (仿射联络, 克里斯托费尔记号), hence geodesics, the curvature tensor and Ricci are all tied to ggg — this is the entire content of "intrinsic geometry".

⚠ Not every Riemannian manifold embeds isometrically into a low-dimensional Euclidean space. Nash's theorem says that it always embeds into some sufficiently high-dimensional RN\mathbb{R}^{N}RN, but even then the "intrinsic" viewpoint is more economical: conclusions should depend only on ggg, not on the embedding.
