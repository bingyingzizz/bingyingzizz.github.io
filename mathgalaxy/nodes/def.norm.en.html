Statement

Let EEE be a vector space over K\mathbb{K}K (K=R\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}K=R or C\mathbb{C}C). A function ∥⋅∥:E→[0,+∞)\|\cdot\| : E \to [0, +\infty)∥⋅∥:E→[0,+∞) is called a norm if

  1. ∥x∥=0  ⟺  x=0\|x\| = 0 \iff x = 0∥x∥=0⟺x=0;
  2. ∥λx∥=∣λ∣ ∥x∥\|\lambda x\| = |\lambda|\,\|x\|∥λx∥=∣λ∣∥x∥;
  3. ∥x+y∥≤∥x∥+∥y∥\|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|∥x+y∥≤∥x∥+∥y∥.

A space equipped with a norm is a normed space. One satisfying only 2 and 3 (allowing nonzero elements to have zero norm) is a seminorm.

Notes

What a norm has beyond a distance is linearity: it records only "how far from the origin", and distances are obtained by translation (see "the distance induced by a norm"). So a normed space is a metric space "with an origin, a scale, and translation invariance".

⚠️ A seminorm is not a norm: one must pass to the quotient by the zero-norm subspace to get a norm (in the construction of LpL^pLp, "∥f∥p=0\|f\|_p = 0∥f∥p​=0 only says f=0f = 0f=0 a.e." is exactly this; see "the LpL^pLp norm").

Which hypothesis each direction uses (the equivalences among the norm axioms and the facts derived from them must be kept separate):

  • Norm   ⟺  \iff⟺ the three axioms: both directions are expansions of the definition, and neither completeness nor finite dimension is needed.
  • x=0  ⟹  ∥x∥=0x=0\implies\|x\|=0x=0⟹∥x∥=0: follows by taking the scalar to be zero in absolute homogeneity; the converse is the positive-definiteness axiom and cannot be derived from homogeneity and the triangle inequality.
  • Seminorm   ⟹  \implies⟹ norm holds only when the zero space is trivial; the zero function on a nontrivial space is a counterexample. Well-definedness of the norm on the quotient by the zero space uses the triangle inequality, not the axiom of choice.
