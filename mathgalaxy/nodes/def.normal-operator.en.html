Statement

Let T:V→VT : V \to VT:V→V be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space.

  • TTT is self-adjoint (called symmetric in the real case and Hermitian in the complex case): T∗=TT^{\ast} = TT∗=T;
  • TTT is normal: T∗T=TT∗T^{\ast}T = T T^{\ast}T∗T=TT∗ (it commutes with its own adjoint);
  • TTT is unitary (orthogonal): T∗T=TT∗=IT^{\ast}T = T T^{\ast} = IT∗T=TT∗=I.

Hierarchy: {\{{unitary}⊂{\} \subset \{}⊂{ normal}\}}, {\{{self-adjoint}⊂{\} \subset \{}⊂{ normal}\}}, and the intersection of the two is "self-adjoint and unitary" (that is, a symmetry with T2=IT^{2} = IT2=I).

Notes

⭐ Why these three words: the spectral theorem holds for normal operators (they can be diagonalized unitarily), and self-adjoint operators are the most important class of normal operators (all eigenvalues real).

General operators cannot do this (Jordan blocks have no orthonormal basis).

⭐ A self-adjoint operator has real eigenvalues, and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal — together these two facts are the content of the spectral theorem.

⚠ "Normal" unifies unitary and self-adjoint only in the complex case: real symmetric (self-adjoint) and real orthogonal (unitary) are special cases of real normal operators, but in the real case they need not be orthogonally diagonalizable (rotation by 90∘90^\circ90∘ is orthogonal and normal, yet has no real eigenvalue) — the spectral theorem in the real case requires symmetry.
