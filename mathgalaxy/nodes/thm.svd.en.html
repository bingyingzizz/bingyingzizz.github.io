Statement

Let A∈Mm×n(R)A \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})A∈Mm×n​(R) (or over C\mathbb{C}C), of rank rrr. Then there exist orthogonal (unitary) matrices U∈MmU \in M_mU∈Mm​, V∈MnV \in M_nV∈Mn​ and a "diagonal" matrix Σ\SigmaΣ (only the first rrr diagonal entries nonzero) such that

A=UΣV†,Σ=diag⁡(σ1,…,σr,0,…),σ1≥⋯≥σr>0A = U \Sigma V^{\dagger}, \qquad \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r, 0, \ldots), \quad \sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0A=UΣV†,Σ=diag(σ1​,…,σr​,0,…),σ1​≥⋯≥σr​>0

The σi\sigma_iσi​ are called the singular values of AAA; they are the square roots of the eigenvalues of A†AA^{\dagger}AA†A (or AA†AA^{\dagger}AA†).

Notes

⭐ Geometric reading of SVD: every linear map = rotation/reflection →\to→ stretch along the coordinate axes →\to→ another rotation/reflection.

Thus it reduces an arbitrary matrix to diagonal form "after choosing good bases UUU and VVV" — this is the complete answer to the equivalence classification (A↦PAQA \mapsto PAQA↦PAQ) (the diagonal entries are the singular values, the number of nonzero ones is the rank).

⭐ Division of labour with the spectral theorem: the spectral theorem handles square and self-adjoint (similarity classification);

SVD handles arbitrary shapes (equivalence classification).

The former requires m=nm = nm=n and A∗=AA^{\ast} = AA∗=A; the latter requires nothing.

⭐ Uses (the most important theorem in numerical linear algebra): low-rank approximation (Eckart–Young: truncating to the first kkk singular values gives the best low-rank approximation), principal component analysis, pseudoinverse, condition number.
