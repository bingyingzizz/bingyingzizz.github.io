Statement

What it does: writes "this quadratic form is positive everywhere" as a matrix condition. In physics it is an "energy form"; in geometry it is another way of writing an inner product.

Let A∈Mn(C)A \in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})A∈Mn​(C) be a Hermitian matrix. Call AAA positive definite if for every x≠0x \ne 0x=0

x∗Ax>0;x^{*}Ax > 0 ;x∗Ax>0;

Replacing >>> by ≥\ge≥ gives positive semidefinite. In the real symmetric case replace x∗x^{*}x∗ by xTx^{T}xT.

Equivalent characterizations (the first two are the most used):

  1. all eigenvalues >0> 0>0 (semidefinite: ≥0\ge 0≥0);
  2. all leading principal minors >0> 0>0 (Sylvester's criterion, thm.sylvester-criterion);
  3. there exists an invertible PPP with A=P∗PA = P^{*}PA=P∗P (semidefinite: A=P∗PA = P^{*}PA=P∗P need not be invertible);
  4. x↦x∗Axx \mapsto \sqrt{x^{*}Ax}x↦x∗Ax​ is a norm (a seminorm in the semidefinite case).

Notes

It is "the matrix of an inner product": the matrix of any inner product in a basis is a positive definite Hermitian matrix; conversely every positive definite matrix defines an inner product. So "positive definite" = "a legitimate inner-product matrix", a correspondence more useful than any criterion.

Why leading principal minors suffice: Sylvester's criterion — in the LDL decomposition the kkk-th pivot equals the ratio of kkk-order leading principal minors, and all pivots are positive exactly when the matrix is positive definite. ⚠️ Only the leading principal minors are checked, not all principal minors (all principal minors >0>0>0 is a stronger condition, equivalent only in special situations).

⭐ Positive definite   ⟹  \implies⟹ admits a square root: there is a unique positive semidefinite A\sqrt{A}A​ with (A)2=A(\sqrt A)^{2} = A(A​)2=A (take square roots of the eigenvalues in an orthonormal basis). This is precisely where A=UPA = UPA=UP and the factor PPP in the polar decomposition come from (thm.polar-decomposition).

⚠️ The sentence "ATAA^{T}AATA is always positive semidefinite" must be written A∗AA^{*}AA∗A over the complex field; and it is positive definite exactly when the columns of AAA are linearly independent (otherwise there is a nonzero kernel vector). This point recurs in regression and least squares.

Hypotheses used in each direction (of the four items, the first two are "spectral/principal minor" criteria and the last two are "decomposition/norm" criteria, with different dependencies):

  • (definition)   ⟺  \iff⟺ (1): uses the spectral theorem (Hermitian matrices are orthogonally diagonalizable, thm.spectral-matrix). After diagonalization x∗Ax=∑λi∣yi∣2x^{*}Ax = \sum \lambda_{i}\lvert y_{i}\rvert^{2}x∗Ax=∑λi​∣yi​∣2, so "always positive"   ⟺  \iff⟺ "every λi>0\lambda_{i} > 0λi​>0". This holds only for Hermitian (real symmetric) matrices: a nonsymmetric matrix can have positive eigenvalues yet fail to be positive definite (the xTAxx^{T}AxxTAx contains a mixed antisymmetric part).
  • (1)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (2): holds only for leading principal minors, not for "all principal minors". Sylvester's criterion says "all nnn leading principal minors are positive"; "all principal minors nonnegative" is the criterion for positive semidefiniteness (the two are not the same; in the semidefinite case a leading principal minor may vanish). This item does not use the spectral theorem, only completing the square — so it is purely algebraic and holds over any ordered field.
  • (2)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (definition): needs real (or Hermitian) symmetry. The method is block Gaussian elimination (LDL decomposition): nonzero leading principal minors guarantee that the elimination can proceed, and the resulting diagonal entries are exactly the ratios of principal minors.
  • (definition)   ⟺  \iff⟺ (3): this step uses the spectral theorem or Cholesky. When A=P∗PA = P^{*}PA=P∗P, x∗Ax=∥Px∥2x^{*}Ax = \lVert Px\rVert^{2}x∗Ax=∥Px∥2; invertibility of PPP gives strict positivity; conversely take P=Λ1/2U∗P = \Lambda^{1/2}U^{*}P=Λ1/2U∗. Note that here one must allow the conjugate transpose for complex/real matrices; writing A=PTPA = P^{T}PA=PTP is correct only in the real symmetric case.
  • (3)   ⟺  \iff⟺ (4): verified directly by the triangle inequality (x∗Ax=∥Px∥\sqrt{x^{*}Ax} = \lVert Px\rVertx∗Ax​=∥Px∥ is a norm). The converse uses the square root defining x∗Axx^{*}Axx∗Ax — one must first show that it is a quadratic form.

⚠️ The three most error-prone points: (a) forgetting the hypothesis "Hermitian/real symmetric" (in the complex case use x∗x^{*}x∗; discussing complex matrices with xTAx>0x^{T}Ax > 0xTAx>0 is meaningless, since it may not be real); (b) replacing "all leading principal minors positive" by "all principal minors positive" — the latter is a stronger condition (also true for positive definite, but in the semidefinite case only the latter can be used); (c) in the semidefinite case PPP is not invertible, eigenvalues may be 000, and the square root is only a seminorm — all three must be relaxed simultaneously.
