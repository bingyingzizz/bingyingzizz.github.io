Statement

A condensed set XXX is quasi-separated   ⟺  \iff⟺ it can be written as

X≅lim→⁡i∈I Si,X \cong \varinjlim_{i \in I}\ S_{i},X≅i∈Ilim​​ Si​,

where each SiS_{i}Si​ is a compact Hausdorff space and each transition map Si↪SjS_{i} \hookrightarrow S_{j}Si​↪Sj​ is an inclusion.

Corollary: the objects of Ind⁡(CHaus)\operatorname{Ind}(\mathbf{CHaus})Ind(CHaus) whose transition maps are injective are exactly the quasi-compact quasi-separated condensed sets.

Notes

⚠️ Do not read this as Ind⁡(CHaus)≅Cond\operatorname{Ind}(\mathbf{CHaus}) \cong \mathrm{Cond}Ind(CHaus)≅Cond. The whole of Ind⁡(CHaus)\operatorname{Ind}(\mathbf{CHaus})Ind(CHaus) is larger than condensed sets: for a general condensed set the transition maps of its compact pieces are merely maps, not inclusions, so only the "inclusion transitions" part matches the quasi-compact quasi-separated objects.

Why this matters. It replaces the abstract finiteness condition "quasi-separated" by an operable description: XXX is the colimit of a sequence of larger and larger compact Hausdorff pieces S1↪S2↪⋯S_{1} \hookrightarrow S_{2} \hookrightarrow \cdotsS1​↪S2​↪⋯.

The language of Ind⁡\operatorname{Ind}Ind-objects is exactly designed for "things assembled as filtered colimits", so many computations in Cond\mathrm{Cond}Cond can be done piece by piece and then colimit-taken.

⭐ This is also the starting point of the later technique "compute sheaf cohomology on compact pieces": compute first on compact Hausdorff pieces, then lift along the filtered colimit (filtered colimits are exact).
