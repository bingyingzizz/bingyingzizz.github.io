Statement

An integral domain AAA is a principal ideal domain (PID) if every ideal is principal: for every ideal III there is aaa such that I=(a)=aAI = (a) = aAI=(a)=aA.

Notes

Examples: Z\mathbb{Z}Z (every ideal is (n)(n)(n)), K[x]K[x]K[x] (ideals are generated by the polynomial of least degree), Z[i]\mathbb{Z}[i]Z[i].

Counterexamples: Z[x]\mathbb{Z}[x]Z[x] is not a PID — (2,x)(2, x)(2,x) is not principal (if (2,x)=(f)(2,x) = (f)(2,x)=(f), then f∣2f \mid 2f∣2 forces fff to be a constant ±1\pm 1±1 or ±2\pm 2±2; the former gives (f)=A(f) = A(f)=A, the latter does not contain xxx, so neither works). Similarly K[x,y]K[x, y]K[x,y], where (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) is not principal.

PID   ⟹  \implies⟹ UFD (imp.ring-pid-ufd) is the central theorem for these rings: existence of factorization rests on "ascending chains of ideals terminate" (Noether), uniqueness on "irreducible = prime" (the Bézout property comes from being a PID).
