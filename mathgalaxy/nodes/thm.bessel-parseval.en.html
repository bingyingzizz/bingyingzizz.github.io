⭐ Reading: Bessel says "coordinate projection does not increase length" (the shadow of xxx on a subspace is no longer than xxx); Parseval says "no direction is lost". Hence completeness   ⟺  \iff⟺ equality — this is turning the geometric fact "is a basis" into a numerical identity.

⚠ For uncountable III, ∑i∈I\sum_{i \in I}∑i∈I​ must be understood as "at most countably many nonzero terms" (see the note on ℓ2(I)\ell^2(I)ℓ2(I)), otherwise the sum is undefined.

Hypotheses used in each direction (the orthonormal square identity links density of the basis with the coefficient identities):

  • Bessel's inequality follows from expanding the square of a finite orthogonal sum and taking the supremum, and does not use completeness of the space; the orthonormality hypothesis cannot be dropped, since repeating the same unit vector double-counts coordinates.
  • (1)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (3): if an orthonormal basis is defined as having closed linear span equal to HHH, density is a definition unfolding, while approximation by Fourier sums uses the best-approximation property of finite orthogonal projections.
  • (2)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (3): the square of the tail of a finite sum equals the total squared norm minus the sum of squares of the coefficients, and Parseval makes the tail tend to zero, with no separate completion needed; (3)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (2) uses continuity of the norm.
  • (3)   ⟹  \implies⟹ (1): every vector is a limit of finite linear combinations, which follows directly from the definition of basis as dense span; if the basis is defined as a maximal orthonormal family, passing to the dense span needs the Hilbert projection theorem.
  • Parseval cannot be guaranteed for an incomplete orthonormal family: in R2\mathbb R^2R2 take only the first coordinate unit vector; all coefficients of the second coordinate unit vector are zero. Uncountable sums are understood as nets over finite subsets.
