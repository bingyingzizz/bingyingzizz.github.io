Statement

What it says: Integrating a (k−1)(k-1)(k−1)-form over the boundary chain gives the same result as integrating its exterior derivative over the chain. This is the common generalization of the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, Gauss's theorem, and the Kelvin–Stokes theorem.

Let ccc be a chain in MMM of dimension kkk (k≥1k\ge 1k≥1), and let ω\omegaω be defined on a neighborhood of the image of ccc and be a (k−1)(k-1)(k−1)-form. Then

∫∂cω=∫cdω.\int_{\partial c}\omega = \int_{c}\mathrm{d}\omega .∫∂c​ω=∫c​dω.

Relation to the manifold version (Stokes' Theorem): The manifold version requires MMM to be compact and orientable with boundary, and ω\omegaω to be defined on MMM. The chain version does not require MMM to be orientable or compact, or ω\omegaω to have compact support. It uses a chain in place of a manifold.

Each is a special case of the other. Decompose (or subdivide) MMM into a covering nnn-chain. Summing the chain formula term by term gives the manifold version.

Conversely, restricting the manifold version to a single orientation-preserving parametrized cube gives the single-cube case of the chain version.

When k=1k = 1k=1 and c=γ:[0,1]→Rc = \gamma : [0,1]\to\mathbb{R}c=γ:[0,1]→R, we have ∂γ=γ(1)−γ(0)\partial\gamma = \gamma(1)-\gamma(0)∂γ=γ(1)−γ(0), and the formula becomes F(γ(1))−F(γ(0))=∫01(F′∘γ) γ′ dtF(\gamma(1))-F(\gamma(0)) = \int_{0}^{1}(F^{\prime}\circ\gamma)\,\gamma^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}tF(γ(1))−F(γ(0))=∫01​(F′∘γ)γ′dt, precisely the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Notes

The proof takes only three lines: ∫cdω=∫Ikc∗dω=∫Ikd(c∗ω)=∫∂Ikc∗ω=∫∂cω\int_{c}\mathrm{d}\omega = \int_{I^{k}}c^{\ast}\mathrm{d}\omega = \int_{I^{k}}\mathrm{d}(c^{\ast}\omega) = \int_{\partial I^{k}}c^{\ast}\omega = \int_{\partial c}\omega∫c​dω=∫Ik​c∗dω=∫Ik​d(c∗ω)=∫∂Ik​c∗ω=∫∂c​ω.

The four steps use, respectively, the definition of integration over a chain, commutation of c∗c^{\ast}c∗ with d\mathrm{d}d, Stokes' Formula on a Single Cube, and the definition and linearity of ∂\partial∂.

The lemma contains the entire difficulty. Here we simply express it in chain language.

Why no orientation is required: For chains, the parametrization already encodes the orientation (Integration over a Chain), so orientability does not enter the statement. An orientation is needed when passing back from the chain version to the manifold version. The signs then come from Sign Convention for Boundaries.

Four in one: k=1k = 1k=1 gives the fundamental theorem of calculus. k=2k = 2k=2 gives Green's theorem. The two forms of k=3k = 3k=3 are Gauss–Ostrogradsky (flux, i.e., the divergence theorem Divergence Theorem) and Kelvin–Stokes (circulation). These are readings of the same theorem for different kkk.

⚠️ Degree and dimension: The degree of ω\omegaω must be one less than the dimension of the chain. When k=0k = 0k=0, we have ∂c=0\partial c = 0∂c=0 and the right-hand side is zero, so the formula reduces to 0=00 = 00=0. Thus the theorem starts at k≥1k\ge 1k≥1.

⚠️ Stating the theorem only for closed chains would be too restrictive: The theorem does not require ccc to be a closed chain. When ∂c≠0\partial c\ne 0∂c=0, the two sides still agree. The vanishing of the pairing between a closed chain and an exact form (∫cdω=0\int_{c}\mathrm{d}\omega = 0∫c​dω=0) is a corollary.
