Statement

Define componentwise addition and matrix multiplication on Mn(K)M_n(K)Mn​(K). Then

  • (Mn(K),+)(M_n(K), +)(Mn​(K),+) is an abelian group (the zero element is the zero matrix);
  • multiplication is associative, distributes over addition on both sides, and has the unit InI_nIn​ (with 111 on the diagonal and 000 elsewhere);
  • hence Mn(K)M_n(K)Mn​(K) is a ring, and moreover a KKK-algebra (addition and scalar multiplication make it a KKK-vector space, and multiplication is compatible with them).

⚠ Multiplication is not commutative (when n≥2n \ge 2n≥2), so it is a noncommutative ring.

Notes

⭐ Every sentence of this theorem is the result of reading Mn(K)M_n(K)Mn​(K) as End⁡(Kn)\operatorname{End}(K^{n})End(Kn): addition of matrices is addition of maps, multiplication is composition, and the unit is the identity map (all proved in the entry "linear map ↔\leftrightarrow↔ matrix").

⭐ Thus Mn(K)M_n(K)Mn​(K) becomes a ring, and one can ask about its ideals, its invertible elements (GL⁡n(K)\operatorname{GL}_n(K)GLn​(K)), one-sided inverses, and so on — the tools of abstract algebra transfer directly.

⚠ "Noncommutative" is essential: the matrix ring is the typical noncommutative ring and the prototype of a finite-dimensional algebra (Wedderburn's theorem says that simple finite-dimensional algebras are Mn(D)M_n(D)Mn​(D)).
