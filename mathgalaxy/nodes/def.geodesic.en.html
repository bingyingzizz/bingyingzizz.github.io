Statement

Let γ:I→S\gamma : I\to Sγ:I→S be a curve on a surface SSS parametrized by arc length (⟨γ˙,γ˙⟩=1\langle \dot\gamma,\dot\gamma\rangle = 1⟨γ˙​,γ˙​⟩=1). γ\gammaγ is a geodesic if its tangent vector is parallel along itself:

A third, equivalent face: any sufficiently short arc of γ\gammaγ is the shortest curve joining its endpoints.

Notes

Division of labour among the three characterizations: ∇γ˙γ˙=0\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma = 0∇γ˙​​γ˙​=0 is the invariant formulation (recognized by every textbook), the geodesic equation is the computational tool (a system of two second-order ODEs), and shortestness is the original geometric motivation (variational). The equivalence of the first two is a pure expansion; for the third see the discussion around Gauss's lemma.

The parameter must be affine: s↦as+bs\mapsto as+bs↦as+b is still a geodesic parameter; after changing to a general parameter ttt the equation acquires an extra term d2sdt2/dsdt\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}s}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\big/\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}t}dt2d2s​/dtds​.

The curve itself does not change; only "constant speed" does — which is why the word "geodesic" always carries an affine parameter.

⚠️ Locally shortest does not mean globally shortest: great circles are geodesics of the sphere, but the arc going more than halfway around is not a shortest connection. Conversely "shortest   ⟹  \implies⟹ geodesic" is true (variational proof), while "geodesic   ⟹  \implies⟹ locally shortest" holds only on sufficiently short arcs (within the injectivity radius of the exponential map).

Why parallel transport preserves length: dds⟨γ˙,γ˙⟩=2⟨∇γ˙γ˙,γ˙⟩\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\langle \dot\gamma,\dot\gamma\rangle = 2\langle \nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma\rangledsd​⟨γ˙​,γ˙​⟩=2⟨∇γ˙​​γ˙​,γ˙​⟩, and on a geodesic this expression vanishes, so the speed is constant and the parameter is necessarily an affine function of arc length.

Every example is worth remembering: geodesics in the plane are straight lines; on the sphere they are great circles; on a surface of revolution they are governed by Clairaut's relation; on a cylinder one simply rolls a straight line of the development back up.
